Purpose, Functions, and Methods

How did we get from people in houses to what we call church today?

Overview

Last session we established what “church” is in the New Testament. This session asks: what is it for, and how did we get from there to here?

Three moves:

  1. A framework — Purpose, Functions, Methods
  2. A history — how the methods developed over 2,000 years
  3. A diagnosis — what organizational and market logics are currently driving the church

By the end, participants should be able to distinguish between what the church must do, what the church has historically invented to do it, and what the church has absorbed from consumer culture.

1. Teaching — The Framework (0:10–0:30)

Note

🗒️ Facilitator note: Write these three words on a whiteboard — PURPOSE, FUNCTIONS, METHODS — with space under each. Do not fill them in. Let the group populate them first. Your job is to ask questions and write what they say.

The three levels

Ask the group:

“Why does the church exist? Not what it does — why does it exist?“

Write their answers. Refine toward something like:

Important

PURPOSE — Why does the church exist?

To become and display God’s redeemed people in Christ, and to participate in God’s mission in the world.

Then ask:

“If that’s why it exists — what does it therefore need to do?“

Write their answers. They will generate most of this list:

Tip

FUNCTIONS — What must the church therefore do?

Worship · Teach · Form disciples · Pray · Share meals and communion · Care for those in need · Love one another · Exercise gifts · Reconcile · Evangelize · Serve · Send

Then ask:

“How does a specific church, in a specific time and place, actually accomplish those things?“

Tip

METHODS — How do we currently do those things?

Sermons · Buildings · Sunday school · Small groups · Worship bands · Youth ministry · Staff · Programs · Sunday services · Mission trips · Websites · Budgets

Then name the key principle:

Important

Purposes are enduring. Functions flow from the purposes. Methods are contextual — they shift with time, culture, and circumstance.

The problem comes when we confuse a method with a function, or a function with the purpose itself.

The purpose question in depth

The New Testament never gives a neat mission statement. You have to synthesize it. Here is one way to organize it:

Purpose Key text
Glorify and display God “Through the church, the manifold wisdom of God should be made known…” — Eph. 3:10
Make disciples “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations…teaching them to obey everything I commanded.” — Matt. 28:19–20
Form people into Christlikeness “…until we all attain…to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.” — Eph. 4:13
Create a new kind of human community “By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you love one another.” — John 13:35
Embody reconciliation “He…has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility.” — Eph. 2:14
Bear witness to God’s kingdom The Acts 2:42–47 community — teaching, fellowship, meals, generosity, prayer, witness
Participate in God’s mission “God…gave us the ministry of reconciliation…We are therefore Christ’s ambassadors.” — 2 Cor. 5:18–20
Note

🗒️ Facilitator note: You do not need to work through all seven. Choose two or three that will resonate most with your group and spend time on those. The goal is to establish that the church’s purpose is richer and harder to measure than attendance, giving, or program quality.

3. Teaching — How the Methods Developed (1:05–1:30)

Note

🗒️ Facilitator note: Present this as a story, not a lecture. Pause after each major shift and ask: “Why do you think this happened? What problem was being solved?” Most of these changes happened for understandable reasons. The question is not “who ruined the church?” It is “what happens when solutions accumulate for 2,000 years?”

The broad arc

Era What the church looked like
1st century Jesus movement → household communities
2nd–3rd centuries Increasing institutionalization: bishops, clergy, liturgy
4th century Constantine + basilicas + professional clergy
Medieval Sacramental church + cathedral + priesthood
Reformation Recovery of Scripture + pulpit elevated + sermon central
Revivalism (1700s–1900s) Preacher / audience model intensified
American voluntary church Consumer choice + denominational competition
Modern evangelical church Market-driven + programmatic + campus-based

One example traced in depth: the sermon

The sermon is a useful case study because it shows clearly how a method develops, becomes central, and eventually defines “church” in people’s minds.

Period What preaching looked like
AD 30–100 Apostolic proclamation + teaching + prophecy + participatory gatherings. “When you come together, each of you has a hymn, a lesson, a revelation…” (1 Cor. 14:26)
AD 100–200 Recognizable homily emerging. Justin Martyr (c. AD 150): Scripture reading, then the president “verbally instructs and exhorts”
AD 200–300 Regular expository preaching established. Origen delivers systematic homilies on biblical books
AD 300–500 Professional clergy preach lengthy sermons to large congregations. Chrysostom — “golden-mouthed” — famous for extended biblical series
Medieval Sermons continue but Eucharist / Mass dominates worship
1500s Reformation Pulpit becomes visually dominant. Scripture exposition and sermon become center of Protestant worship
1700s–1900s Revivalism further develops preacher / audience model
1900s–today Modern evangelical: worship music + 30–45 minute sermon as the primary product
Important

The most defensible conclusion:

Preaching is ancient and thoroughly Christian.

Sermon-centered worship — where the extended monologue by a professional pastor becomes the organizing center of the weekly gathering — is much later.

The question worth asking: “How did one particular form of teaching come to dominate the gathering so completely that many Christians now instinctively think that is what ‘church’ is?“

The same pattern applies to other elements

The word “pastor”

In many American churches: “the pastor” = primary preacher + CEO + organizational leader + spiritual authority + public face.

That is not an obvious New Testament category.

Poimēn (shepherd/pastor) appears as a ministry term in Ephesians 4:11. But the far more common NT leadership language is elder (presbyteros) and overseer (episkopos), generally in the plural.

The development:

elders among the congregation → clergy offices → priest / bishop → Reformation minister → professional pastor → modern senior pastor

The building

The earliest Christians met in homes for several centuries. By the third century, adapted buildings appear (Dura-Europos, c. AD 240). After Constantine, large basilicas become Christian worship spaces — and architecture begins changing people’s understanding of church.

Instead of “The church is gathering at Lydia’s house” you increasingly get “The Christians are gathering at the basilica.”

Eventually the building itself gets called “the church.” Language naturally evolves — but it can subtly change theology.

4. Discussion — Community Logic vs. Organizational Logic (1:30–1:50)

Note

🗒️ Facilitator note: This is the most personally charged section. The goal is not to condemn churches but to help participants name something they may have felt without having language for it.

Put this table up and ask participants to respond to it:

Community logic Organizational logic
Belonging Attendance
Formation Programming
Participation Consumption
Gifts Roles
Relationship Efficiency
Faithfulness Growth
Disciple Attendee
Shepherd Executive
Mission Brand
One another Audience
Tip

Discussion questions:

  1. Which column feels more familiar from your church experience? Not which one you prefer — which one you have actually encountered.

  2. A church can unconsciously operate this feedback loop: produces religious experiences → attendees consume them → successful churches attract more consumers → revenue funds better programs → better programs attract more consumers. Have you experienced that? What did it feel like?

  3. Some of the discomfort people feel with church may not be rejection of the church at all. It may be grief over the distance between the church they long for and the church they have experienced. Does that distinction land for anyone?

  4. What would a church look like that genuinely operated by the community column rather than the organizational column? Have you seen it? Is it realistic?

  1. Which column feels more familiar from your experience?

  2. The teaching described a feedback loop where churches produce religious experiences that people consume, which drives growth, which funds better production. Have you experienced that? What did it feel like from the inside?

  3. Some discomfort with church may not be rejection of the church but grief over the distance between the church we long for and the church we have experienced. Does that land?

  4. What would it look like for a church to genuinely operate by community logic rather than organizational logic? Is that realistic at scale?

Close (1:50–2:00)

“We’ve now established what the church is, what it’s for, and how we got from the New Testament to now. The next sessions get harder — we’re going to talk about authority, about harm, and about how to tell the difference between the two.

But before we do, I want to name something. The cynicism a lot of us carry about the church is not just disappointment. It’s often a sign that we knew — or hoped — the church was supposed to be something more than it has been. That intuition is worth taking seriously.”

Tip

Seed question for the week:

Think about your current or most recent church experience. Using the Purpose → Functions → Methods framework:

  • What methods did that church prioritize most?
  • What functions did those methods actually serve?
  • And underneath it all — what purpose did the church seem to be organized around?

Were those three things aligned?
