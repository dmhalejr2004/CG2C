Session 3 – Purpose, Functions, and Methods

Starting with a framework

Last session we asked what the church is. This session asks what it is for – and then traces how we got from there to what we experience today.

A useful way to think about this has three levels. The first is purpose – why does the church exist at all? The second is functions – what does it therefore need to do? The third is methods – how does it actually do those things in a specific time and place?

The distinction matters because purposes are enduring, functions flow from the purposes, and methods are contextual. Methods shift with time, culture, and circumstance. That is not a problem. The problem comes when we confuse a method with a function, or a function with the purpose itself. A lot of the frustration people carry about the church lives right in that confusion.

Why does the church exist?

The New Testament never gives us a clean corporate mission statement. You have to synthesize it from many different texts and images. But when you do, something like this emerges:

The church exists to become and display God’s redeemed people in Christ, and to participate in God’s mission in the world.

That is broader than “save people” or “worship God” or “make disciples.” Those are all parts of it. Here is what each piece actually means.

To display God

Paul says in Ephesians 3:10-21 that God’s intent is that “through the church, the manifold wisdom of God should be made known.”

That is an extraordinary claim. The church is not merely an organization that tells people about God. The church itself is supposed to be evidence of what God is doing. People who ordinarily would not belong together – rich and poor, different races, former enemies – become one new people reconciled to God and to one another. The community becomes part of the message.

To make disciples

This is the most familiar outward mission: “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them and teaching them to obey everything I have commanded you” (Matthew 28:19-20).

Notice that Jesus does not say “make converts.” He says make disciples. Evangelism is the beginning, not the endpoint. The goal is people who actually follow Jesus – hear the gospel, believe, are baptized, are taught, and grow into obedience.

To form people into Christlikeness

Ephesians 4 may be the clearest description of what the church is supposed to produce. Christ gives apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers “to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ – until we all attain to mature manhood, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ” (Ephesians 4:11-13).

Two things stand out there. First, the leaders do not actually do the ministry in Paul’s description – they equip God’s people to do it. Second, the desired output is not attendance. It is maturity. The New Testament’s scorecard for the church is something like: are these people increasingly becoming like Jesus?

To create a new kind of human community

Jesus did not simply save isolated individuals. He created a people. Peter calls Christians “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation” (1 Peter 2:9). Paul calls the church a body, a household, a temple, a bride.

And Jesus gives this community a defining characteristic: “By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you love one another” (John 13:35). The church’s internal life becomes part of its external witness. What the community is – not only what it says – is part of the message.

To embody reconciliation

Ephesians 2 may be the most important church passage in the New Testament. Christ does not merely reconcile individuals vertically to God. He breaks down the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile and creates “one new humanity” (Ephesians 2:15).

Reconciliation with God produces reconciliation with one another. Racial, ethnic, economic, and social barriers being overcome within the Christian community are not peripheral social projects added onto the real gospel. They are supposed to be manifestations of the gospel itself – visible evidence that something has actually changed.

To bear witness to God’s kingdom

The Acts 2:42-47 community is striking. The early Christians do not merely preach a message. They become a strange kind of community: teaching, fellowship, shared meals, prayer, generosity, worship, care, and witness all woven together. And Acts says outsiders noticed. Their life together authenticated their proclamation.

To participate in God’s mission

Paul puts it this way: “God reconciled us to himself through Christ and gave us the ministry of reconciliation… We are therefore Christ’s ambassadors” (2 Corinthians 5:18-20). The church does not exist primarily to preserve itself. It exists for God’s mission.

What the church must therefore do

If those are the purposes, the functions follow. These are the things the church must do in some form regardless of time or place – worship, teach, form disciples, pray, share the Lord’s Supper, care for those in need, love one another, exercise gifts, reconcile, evangelize, serve, and send. They are not optional. They are what it means to be a church in any era.

The methods – contextual and changeable

Then there is the third layer: the specific ways a particular church in a particular time and place actually accomplishes those functions. Sermons, buildings, Sunday school, small groups, worship bands, youth ministry, staff, programs, Sunday services, mission trips, websites, budgets, denominations – all of these are methods.

Most of them exist because someone had a real problem and invented a real solution. The question is what happens over time, when the solutions accumulate and eventually start to feel like the definition of church itself.

It is worth thinking about common church practices in three rough categories. The first is things described or commanded in the New Testament – gathering, teaching, prayer, the Lord’s Supper, baptism, singing, giving, care for the poor, fellowship, mutual encouragement, evangelism, the exercise of spiritual gifts, elders, deacons, and church discipline. These are the core.

The second category is useful historical methods – large dedicated buildings, the 30-45 minute weekly sermon, Sunday morning as the primary service, seminary-trained clergy, denominations, Sunday school, the full-time senior pastor, children’s and youth ministries, the worship band. None of these are commanded in the New Testament, but they developed for real reasons and have served real purposes.

The third category is what we might call the modern consumer layer – multiple Sunday services, church branding and logos, marketing campaigns, attraction-oriented services, professional production and lighting, attendance as the primary metric of success, the pastor as CEO or celebrity, and the congregation primarily as an audience.

One important caution: “not commanded” does not mean “bad.” A church building is enormously useful. A seminary can train people to interpret Scripture carefully. The issue is not that second-category things exist. The issue is what happens when we mistake a useful historical form for the biblical definition of church – or when the third category begins distorting the first two.

How the methods developed

Most of the changes in church history happened for understandable reasons. The question is not who ruined the church. It is what happens when solutions accumulate across 2,000 years and eventually become our unconscious definition of what church is.

Era What the church looked like
1st century Jesus movement, household communities
2nd-3rd centuries Increasing institutionalization: bishops, clergy, liturgy
4th century Constantine, basilicas, professional clergy, large congregations
Medieval Sacramental church, cathedral, priesthood, Mass
Reformation Scripture recovered, pulpit elevated, sermon becomes central
Revivalism (1700s-1900s) Preacher-audience model intensified
American voluntary church Consumer choice, denominational competition
Modern evangelical church Market-driven, programmatic, campus-based

The sermon is a useful example to trace in some detail, because it shows clearly how a method develops, becomes central, and eventually starts to define what “church” means in people’s minds.

In the New Testament, preaching and teaching are everywhere. Jesus preached. Peter preached at Pentecost. Paul preached extensively. But there is an important distinction between public proclamation of the gospel and what happened when an established congregation gathered. The Greek word kerysso – to proclaim or herald – frequently describes announcing the gospel to outsiders. Within gathered churches, the New Testament uses language of teaching, exhortation, prophecy, Scripture reading, and mutual participation. First Corinthians 14:26 says: “When you come together, each of you has a hymn, or a word of instruction, a revelation…” Colossians 3:16 says “teach and admonish one another.” The overall picture is more participatory than the modern sermon-centered service.

By around AD 150, something closer to a recognizable sermon is emerging. Justin Martyr describes Christians gathering on Sunday, reading from the memoirs of the apostles and the prophets, and then the president “verbally instructs and exhorts the congregation to imitate these good things.” Then prayer, then Communion, then care for the poor. It is beginning to look familiar – but the Eucharist still occupies a central place alongside the teaching.

By the third century, Origen is delivering systematic expository homilies on biblical books, and we still have large collections of his sermons. By the fourth and fifth centuries you have John Chrysostom – whose nickname literally means “golden-mouthed” – Augustine of Hippo, Ambrose of Milan, and Gregory of Nazianzus. Extended exposition of Scripture by a recognized clergy member is now firmly established.

Constantine’s legalization of Christianity in AD 313 matters here too. Christians increasingly gathered in large basilicas rather than in homes and smaller spaces, and that physical shift had consequences. A gathering of 20-50 people in a home accommodates interaction naturally. A gathering of hundreds or thousands in a basilica naturally encourages a clergy-to-congregation flow rather than a believer-to-believer one. The architecture started shaping the theology.

The biggest step toward the modern Protestant service comes in the 16th century Reformation. Luther, Calvin, Zwingli, and the other Reformers reacted against a medieval Catholic worship structure centered heavily on the Mass and dramatically elevated the proclamation and exposition of Scripture. In many Protestant churches the pulpit became visually dominant. Revivalism in the 18th and 19th centuries intensified the preacher-audience model further, and the modern evangelical service – worship music plus a 30-45 minute sermon as the principal event – is the result of that long development.

Period What preaching looked like
AD 30-100 Apostolic proclamation, teaching, prophecy, participatory gatherings
AD 100-200 Recognizable homily emerging
AD 200-300 Regular expository preaching established
AD 300-500 Professional clergy preaching long sermons to large congregations
Medieval Sermons continue; Eucharist-centered worship dominates
Reformation Scripture exposition and sermon become the center of Protestant worship
Revivalism Preacher-audience model intensified
Today Worship music plus 30-45 minute sermon as primary event

The most honest conclusion is this: preaching is ancient and thoroughly Christian. Sermon-centered worship – where the extended monologue by a professional pastor becomes the organizing center of the weekly gathering – is much later. The interesting question is not whether the sermon is good. It often is. The question is how one particular form of teaching came to dominate the gathering so completely that many Christians now instinctively think that is what church is.

The same pattern applies to other elements. In many American churches today, “the pastor” means the primary preacher, the CEO, the organizational leader, the spiritual authority, and the public face of the congregation. That is not an obvious New Testament category. The word poimen – shepherd or pastor – appears as a ministry term most famously in Ephesians 4:11, but the far more common New Testament leadership language is elder (presbyteros) and overseer (episkopos), and these almost always appear in the plural. The development from elders among the congregation to developing clergy offices to the Reformation minister to the modern senior pastor spans centuries and was shaped by forces well beyond the New Testament text.

The building follows a similar arc. The earliest Christians met in homes for several centuries. By the third century they were adapting buildings specifically for worship – the Dura-Europos house church, dating to roughly AD 240, is an important archaeological example. After Constantine, large basilicas become Christian worship spaces and the architecture begins changing people’s understanding of what church is. Instead of “the church is gathering at Lydia’s house,” you increasingly get “the Christians are gathering at the basilica.” Eventually the building itself gets called the church. Language naturally evolves. But it can subtly shift theology.

Community logic versus organizational logic

This brings us to the current moment. Not all of what drives modern churches is ancient tradition. Some of it has been absorbed directly from contemporary American culture – specifically from consumer capitalism and organizational management.

Churches are not businesses. But they can unconsciously begin operating like them.

Community logic Organizational logic
Belonging Attendance
Formation Programming
Participation Consumption
Gifts Roles
Relationship Efficiency
Faithfulness Growth
Disciple Attendee
Shepherd Executive
Mission Brand
One another Audience

Neither column is entirely good or bad. Churches inevitably have some organization, and organization is not inherently evil. The question is which logic is primary – which column actually drives decisions, structures, and how success gets measured.

A church operating primarily in the right column can develop a particular feedback loop: it produces religious experiences, attendees consume them, successful churches attract more consumers, increased attendance produces increased revenue, increased revenue funds better programs and facilities and staff, and better offerings attract more consumers. That is a very different feedback loop from the one the New Testament describes.

Consider the vocabulary that has developed for talking about church. “We need to attract young families.” “People really like our worship.” “We’re trying to grow attendance.” “What does your church offer for teenagers?” “We’re losing people to the church down the road.” None of those sentences necessarily indicates something terrible. But taken together they reveal a marketplace vocabulary. The congregation becomes a customer base. The church becomes a provider. The pastor becomes an executive. The worship team becomes content production. The sermon becomes the principal product. Programs become product lines. Attendance becomes market share.

When that logic takes hold, the third category begins distorting the first two. The methods start reshaping the functions, and the functions start obscuring the purpose.

The deeper question

Paul never conceptualizes people as going to the church. The people come together and thereby constitute the gathered church. That is a significant difference from how we talk today.

The New Testament’s scorecard for the church is not attendance, giving, number of campuses, size of staff, quality of programs, quality of preaching, or quality of music. Those things are not necessarily bad. Some may help accomplish the mission. But none of them is the mission.

The New Testament asks different questions. Are people becoming like Jesus? Are they loving one another sacrificially? Are barriers between people being overcome? Are ordinary believers being equipped to minister? Are the poor and vulnerable being cared for? Are people becoming disciples who make disciples? Does this community make the character of God visible to the surrounding world?

Those are much closer to what the New Testament actually cares about when it evaluates a church.

Questions worth sitting with

  1. Using the purpose, functions, methods framework – what purpose does the Evangelical American Church seem to be organized around? What methods does it prioritize? Are those two things aligned?

  2. Which column of community logic versus organizational logic feels more familiar from your experience? What are the implications?

  3. Some of the discomfort people feel with church may not be rejection of the church at all. It may be grief over the distance between the church they long for and the church they have experienced. Does that distinction land for you?

  4. What would a church actually look like that operated by community logic rather than organizational logic? Have you seen it? Is it realistic at scale?
