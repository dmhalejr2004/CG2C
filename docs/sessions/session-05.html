When Leadership Goes Wrong

From red flags to real questions – what to do, who is responsible, and what healing looks like

From the flags to the question nobody wants

Last session ended with the red flag / green flag table. Most people find that exercise easier than expected. The flags are legible once you name them. Then comes the harder question: if I see those things, what do I actually do?

That is the question most people carry but rarely ask out loud, because the answers are uncomfortable. They require naming things in communities we love, about people we may have trusted for years. They require acknowledging that we might have stayed longer than we should have, or that we saw something and said nothing, or that we said something and nothing changed.

And there is one more layer of difficulty. The flags are easy to see in retrospect. In the moment, they are almost always harder. You are inside the community. The relationships are real. The good that has happened is real. The person you are questioning has also done things that mattered to you. That context does not make the flags less real, but it does make them harder to act on. Understanding that is not an excuse. It is just honest.

This session tries to sit with the questions that follow.

Naming what you are dealing with: a spectrum of problems

Not every difficult experience in a church is the same kind of thing. The response that fits one category can be harmful if applied to another. So the first task is learning to name what you are actually dealing with.

Disagreement about methods happens when people hold different preferences about how the church does things – worship style, programming, staffing decisions, budget priorities, how meetings are run. These are real disagreements. They can be sharp. But they are disagreements about means, not ends. Nobody’s character is at stake. Nobody is being harmed.

Disagreement about principles or direction is more serious because the stakes are higher. It happens when people disagree about what the church should be doing or where it is headed – theological emphasis, mission priorities, how the church relates to its neighborhood or denomination. These disagreements can threaten a community’s coherence and deserve serious engagement. But they are still disagreements between people with different convictions, not failures of character or conduct.

Church hurt is pain that comes from being part of an imperfect human community. It happens when a leader makes a bad call that affects you. When a community fails to support you in a moment you needed it. When you are overlooked, misunderstood, or handled poorly. When someone says something careless that lodges in you for years. Church hurt is real. It is significant. It does not require bad intent to cause real damage. And it is nearly universal among people who have been part of churches for any length of time.

Church abuse is categorically different. Abuse happens when someone in a position of spiritual authority uses that authority to control, exploit, harm, or silence people. It may be sexual, financial, spiritual, or emotional. The key element is not just that something bad happened – it is that power was used against someone. What makes it abuse is the exploitation of a power differential.

A framework for naming what happened

Category What it involves Who is affected What it requires
Disagreement about methods Different preferences about how things are done Community cohesion Honest conversation; willingness to compromise or separate
Disagreement about principles Different convictions about what the church should be Community direction Serious engagement; possibly agreed separation
Church hurt Imperfect community causing real pain The person hurt Acknowledgment; accountability; time
Church abuse Power used against vulnerable people The person harmed Outside accountability; professional support; possibly civil authorities

The harder distinction: church hurt vs. church abuse

These get conflated constantly, and the conflation does harm in both directions. Treating hurt as if it were abuse inflates the category. Treating abuse as if it were hurt minimizes it and leaves people without the response they need.

Church hurt Church abuse
Power dynamic May or may not involve a power difference Always involves exploitation of a power difference
Intent Usually no intent to harm Intent is not what determines abuse; harm caused by power use is what matters
Pattern May be a one-time failure Often a pattern; single incidents can still be abuse
Leader’s response Leader may acknowledge and change Leader typically denies, minimizes, or re-frames
Appropriate response Internal to community; accountability; relationship repair Outside accountability; professional care; civil authorities when laws are broken
What the person harmed needs To be heard and acknowledged Safety first; then support; then accountability for the perpetrator

Why does it hurt so much when church leaders fail?

This question deserves more than a brief answer, because the depth of the pain surprises people – including the people experiencing it.

When a trusted pastor or spiritual mentor fails, people often report that it was more devastating than other betrayals – more disorienting, longer-lasting, more threatening to their sense of self. There are several reasons for this, and understanding them does not make the pain smaller, but it can make it less confusing.

The trust involved is unusually deep. People come to churches during their most vulnerable moments – grief, crisis, searching, the disorientation of major life transitions. They extend a level of trust to church leaders they would not extend in most other contexts, precisely because the relationship is framed as something more than professional. When that trust is broken, the wound is deep in proportion to the openness that preceded it.

Spiritual authority is a uniquely powerful kind of authority. Most positions of authority carry influence in a bounded domain. A spiritual authority is different. When someone is understood as representing God, speaking for God, or carrying God’s care, that relationship reaches into things that other relationships do not reach – character questions, life direction, ultimate meaning. A failure there does not stay in one lane the way a professional disappointment does.

The failure raises theological questions, not just relational ones. This is what makes church hurt theologically destabilizing in ways that other kinds of hurt are not. When a spiritual authority fails, people often find themselves asking: If this person was not who they appeared to be, what does that say about the God they claimed to represent? If I trusted my discernment and got it wrong, can I trust myself? If the community that shaped my faith was built on something false, is the faith itself false? These are not irrational questions. They are the predictable result of a specific kind of betrayal – one where the human and the divine were fused together in ways the New Testament actually warns against.

The community often becomes a second wound. In most cases of significant leadership failure, some portion of the congregation rallies around the leader. The people who should be a resource for healing become a second source of harm. The person who has been harmed loses their community at the exact moment they most need it. The social cost of naming what happened can be the loss of every relationship built in that setting.

We are wired for belonging. Psychological research consistently shows that social exclusion and rejection activate the same neural pathways as physical pain. Being pushed out of a community – or watching everyone you know choose the person who harmed you – is not merely emotionally uncomfortable. It registers as something close to injury.

What should I do?

When you see a red flag

Seeing a red flag does not automatically require action. What it requires is honest attention. Before deciding what to do, it helps to ask a prior question: what am I actually seeing?

Our experiences shape our perceptions. Someone who grew up with an authoritarian parent may read a firm leader as threatening when the situation does not warrant it. Someone who has been in a controlling church may read ordinary structure as a red flag. This is not weakness. It is how human beings work. Our histories come with us.

At the same time, the opposite error is equally common: explaining away things we see because we love the community, because the leader has done genuine good in our lives, because naming the thing would cost us something. Both kinds of distortion are available to us.

A useful check: what would I think about this if this person were not my pastor? What would I tell a friend who described this situation to me? That kind of imaginative distance can be genuinely clarifying.

When the concern is yours to address

If you have a concern about a leader or a community, the most uncomfortable step is usually the most productive one: direct conversation. Matthew 18 describes a process of direct engagement before escalation, and the principle behind it is sound even when the specific mechanics need contextual judgment. Going directly to someone about a concern – clearly, without accusation, with openness to being wrong – is both the most respectful and the most productive path when the relationship and the concern make it possible.

It is not always possible. When the concern involves abuse, or when there is a significant power differential, or when previous attempts at direct conversation have been punished, direct engagement is not the operating framework. The point is not to follow a formula. The point is to ask whether direct engagement is available before bypassing it.

When you need to check your own motives

This is difficult to say, but it needs to be said: not every conflict with a leader is a leadership failure. Some of it is genuine disagreement. Some of it is a mismatch between what a community is and what someone needed it to be. Some of it is accumulated personal history expressing itself through a present frustration.

None of that means the concern is not real. But it means honest self-examination is part of a responsible response. The question is not whether you have been hurt – you may genuinely have been. The question is whether the story you are telling about it is the only story available, or whether there are things about your own experience worth understanding as part of the picture.

This is not an invitation to dismiss your own perception. It is an invitation to hold it with some humility.

What should the church do?

A community’s response to leadership problems is one of the most important tests of its actual health. What a church says it believes about accountability and what it does when accountability is required are often very different things.

For disagreements about methods or direction: The community’s job is to build and maintain real structures for honest disagreement – forums where concerns can be raised without consequence, leaders who can receive criticism without treating it as disloyalty, a culture where “I think we are heading in the wrong direction” can be said without social penalty. Most churches do not have this. Its absence does not make a church abusive, but it creates conditions in which smaller problems become larger ones.

For church hurt: The community’s job is acknowledgment. A person who has been hurt needs to hear that what happened to them was real, that it mattered, and that the community takes responsibility for its part. This is consistently avoided because acknowledgment feels like liability, and communities are often more interested in self-protection than in repair. A community that handles hurt well will have leaders who can say “I got that wrong” or “we failed you there” without it dissolving the community.

For moral failure or abuse: The community’s responsibility is more demanding. It requires immediate removal of the person from the role where they can continue to cause harm; transparent communication with the community about what has happened, in terms that protect those harmed while being honest about the nature of the failure; active care for those harmed – not just pastoral outreach but practical support and professional resources; outside accountability rather than handling the situation internally; and commitment to a real process, not a managed narrative.

The failure mode here is almost always the same: the community prioritizes its reputation, its continuity, and the leader’s comfort over the needs of those who were harmed. Institutions do this predictably. Knowing that it is predictable is the first step toward doing something different.

What should the leader do?

When a concern is raised: A leader who receives a concern about their behavior has two basic options. They can treat it as a threat, or they can treat it as information. A leader who treats concerns as threats will manage them – explain them away, marginalize the person raising them, frame the concern as a spiritual problem in the one raising it, or use loyalty as the measure of faithfulness. Over time this isolates the leader inside a circle of people who only say what the leader wants to hear.

A leader who treats concerns as information will receive them, sit with them, take them seriously even when they are partially wrong, and demonstrate that raising a concern is safe. Over time this builds the kind of community where small problems get addressed before they become large ones.

On the higher standard: The New Testament is explicit that leadership in the church carries a higher standard than ordinary membership. James 3:1 – “Not many of you should become teachers, my fellow believers, because you know that we who teach will be judged more strictly.” The qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 describe character requirements that are prerequisites, not aspirational targets. A leader can fail in a specific moment and still be qualified to lead. But a sustained pattern of behavior that contradicts these qualifications is a question about fitness for the role, not just personal failure.

The tension here is real: leaders are human and will fail like humans do, and we should not expect otherwise. But the office they hold creates higher accountability. Both things are true at the same time.

On responsibility: A leader who has caused harm cannot transfer responsibility for that harm to the people who were harmed. This sounds obvious stated directly. But it happens constantly in language like: they came to me with their problems, they misunderstood what I was doing, the enemy is using this to attack the ministry. This kind of language redistributes blame. It does not diminish the leader’s responsibility. A leader who caused harm is responsible for the harm they caused, regardless of the circumstances surrounding it.

What do you do when nobody does anything?

This is the scenario many people in this room may be living with. You saw something. You said something, or you did not. Nothing changed.

You cannot fix it. You cannot force a leader to acknowledge failure. You cannot force a community to recognize something it is not ready to see. Some situations do not resolve. Accepting this is not giving up. It is releasing yourself from a responsibility that was never yours to carry. You are not responsible for other people’s behavior. You are not responsible for an institution’s response. You are responsible for your own choices: what you say, what you participate in, and what you are willing to put your name next to.

You can name what you experienced. Telling your own story – clearly, accurately, without exaggeration – is something you have the right to do regardless of whether anyone responds. The idea that survivors of harm owe silence to the institutions that harmed them is not a Christian value, even when it is sometimes expressed in Christian language.

External accountability is a legitimate path. Denominational structures, outside investigators, civil authorities, journalists, and communities of survivors have historically been the primary mechanisms that produced accountability in church contexts where internal accountability failed. If internal paths have been exhausted, external ones are legitimate.

What to do with people you love who are still inside: The people you care about who are still following a leader you have concerns about are making their own choices. You cannot extract someone from a community they have chosen. Attempts to do so tend to push people closer to the community rather than toward the door. What you can do is stay in honest relationship without demanding agreement, ask questions rather than making accusations, be a steady presence that does not require the other person to validate your perception before you will care about them. And accept that some people will stay longer than you think they should – and that is not your failure.

Forgiveness and consequences – two things that are not the same

This is one of the most important distinctions in this session, and it is one that gets used against harm survivors with some regularity.

Forgiveness is a decision the harmed person makes about their own interior. It is the process of releasing the debt you are owed – not pretending it was not owed, not declaring the harm unreal, not automatically restoring the relationship. It is a decision to stop organizing your life around the debt. Psychologically and spiritually, forgiveness tends to benefit the person forgiving as much as the person forgiven.

Consequences are what happen in the external world as a result of someone’s choices. They exist independently of forgiveness. A person can be genuinely forgiven and still face the consequences of what they did. These are not in tension. Forgiveness does not remove consequences. Consequences do not indicate unforgiveness.

The confusion becomes harmful when institutions or leaders use forgiveness language to argue against accountability. “Aren’t we called to forgive?” is not an argument against a police report. “He has asked for forgiveness” is not an argument for restoring someone to authority over vulnerable people. These categories operate in different domains.

Restoration – two very different things

The New Testament consistently orients toward restoration rather than permanent exclusion. Galatians 6:1 – “restore that person gently.” 2 Corinthians 2 urges reaffirming love for a person who has been disciplined. This is a real value. But restoration means two different things, and collapsing them into one does harm.

Restoration to community means full membership, belonging, love, and participation in the life of the church. It means not being permanently defined by your worst failure. This is always the goal, and it should be pursued unless someone’s ongoing presence poses a real danger to others.

Restoration to leadership means returning to a position of authority over other people, particularly over vulnerable people who extend trust. This is a separate question, and the answer is not automatic.

Restoration to community Restoration to leadership
Who benefits The restored person and the community Must also benefit those who would be placed under their authority
What it requires Genuine acknowledgment; demonstrated change Acknowledgment; sustained change over years; rebuilt trust; demonstrated fitness to hold power responsibly
Who decides The community together Significant weight given to those who were harmed
Default posture This is the goal and should be pursued Must be earned; may not be appropriate
Timeline Can begin after genuine accountability Requires years, not months

Any process that restores a leader to a platform within months of a disclosed failure should be viewed with significant skepticism. Genuine character change, rebuilding of trust, and demonstrated transformation take years.

What is permanently disqualifying from leadership

The New Testament qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 describe a standard of being above reproach and having a good reputation with outsiders. Some failures so thoroughly violate this standard that the trust required for pastoral authority cannot be meaningfully rebuilt – not as punishment, but as protection for people who would be placed under that authority.

Categories that are almost certainly permanently disqualifying:

  • Sexual abuse of minors
  • Sexual coercion of or abuse of adult congregants or ministry staff
  • Sustained financial fraud against the congregation
  • A documented pattern of spiritual abuse – systematic use of religious authority to harm people
  • Conviction for violent crime

A person in one of these categories can be genuinely repentant, genuinely restored to community, genuinely forgiven. None of those things automatically restores fitness for leadership. We do not require that a disbarred attorney be reinstated to prove that their repentance is sincere. The same logic applies here.

New Testament principles worth holding onto

Several passages come up in these situations and their actual meaning matters.

Matthew 18:15-17 describes a process of direct engagement before escalation. The principle behind it is sound: deal with things as directly and locally as possible first. But this process was designed for conflict between people with roughly equivalent standing. It was not written to tell someone who has been abused that they must first go alone to confront their abuser.

1 Timothy 5:19-20 – “Do not entertain an accusation against an elder unless it is brought by two or three witnesses. But those elders who are sinning you are to reprove before everyone, so that the others may take warning.” Accusations need corroboration. But confirmed failure is addressed publicly, not privately managed to protect the institution’s reputation.

James 3:1 – “Not many of you should become teachers… because we who teach will be judged more strictly.” The higher standard for leaders is not an add-on. It is built into the New Testament’s understanding of leadership.

Galatians 6:1-2 – “If someone is caught in a sin, you who live by the Spirit should restore that person gently. But watch yourselves, or you also may be tempted. Carry each other’s burdens.” Restoration is the goal. Gentleness is the manner. But the verse begins with acknowledging that the person has been caught in a sin – not explaining it away.

1 Corinthians 5:1-5 – Paul is sharp about a situation where the church at Corinth is proud of itself for tolerating behavior that is clearly harmful. His concern is not that the community has failed to forgive. His concern is that they have failed to take the harm seriously. Community loyalty that protects harm is not a virtue in the New Testament.

What does healthy leadership look like?

This class could leave people with the impression that church leadership is primarily a problem to be managed. That is not the picture the New Testament paints. Healthy leadership exists. It is worth being able to describe it.

It is plural. The New Testament consistently describes church leadership in the plural – elders, not an elder. Plural leadership creates mutual accountability, distributes wisdom, and makes it much harder for any single person’s blind spots to become the community’s blind spots. A church where one person holds all meaningful authority has already removed one of the primary structural safeguards the New Testament builds in.

It is transparent about its own limitations. Healthy leaders know what they do not know. They can say “I got that wrong” without it undermining their effectiveness. They can receive criticism without treating it as betrayal. They distinguish between their own preferences and God’s will, and they make that distinction out loud.

It develops other people. Ephesians 4:11-12 describes leaders as people whose job is to equip others for ministry – not to do the ministry while others watch. A healthy leader is oriented toward building capacity in the people around them rather than building dependence on themselves.

It is accountable to structures outside itself. This can be a denomination, an independent elder board, a formal accountability relationship with other leaders outside the congregation, or some combination. What matters is that the accountability has teeth – that there is someone who can say “that was wrong” and have it mean something.

It creates safety for honest disagreement. The test is not whether people agree with the leader. It is whether people can safely disagree.

It distinguishes between the mission and the leader. The community’s identity does not fuse with the leader’s identity. The vision belongs to God and the community, not to the individual currently serving. A leader who has cultivated this knows that the work will go on without them, and they consider that a success rather than a threat.

Questions worth sitting with

  1. Which of the four categories – methods, principles, church hurt, church abuse – describes most of your difficult experiences with the church? Does naming it change anything?

  2. Is there a situation where you saw something, said nothing, and still wonder about that choice? What would you do differently now?

  3. What does the distinction between forgiveness and consequences mean to you personally? Is there a place where you have conflated them, in either direction?

  4. What would genuinely healthy church leadership look like in a community you could actually imagine being part of? What would you need to see to trust it?

  5. Is there someone in your life who has been more seriously harmed by a church than you have – who needs something different from you than agreement or advice? What do they actually need?
