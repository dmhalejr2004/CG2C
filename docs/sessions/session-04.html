Leadership in the New Testament

What roles actually exist · What they require · How they fail · How to tell the difference

Where we are

The last two sessions established what the church is and what it is for. This session narrows the focus to one of the most contested and consequential questions in that conversation: what does the New Testament actually say about leadership?

This matters for this class in particular because a significant amount of church hurt originates in leadership – in the misuse of authority, in leaders who did not meet the standards they claimed to hold, in communities that had no mechanism for accountability when things went wrong. Before we can name those failures accurately, we need to know what the New Testament actually describes. What roles does it establish? What does it require of the people in those roles? What does it say about what happens when leaders fail? And what guidance does it give for recognizing the difference between genuine and false leadership?

The leadership roles the New Testament actually describes

It is worth starting with what is actually there, because the modern church has often imported organizational structures from the surrounding culture and given them biblical names. The New Testament describes a smaller and more specific set of roles than most contemporary churches use.

Apostle

The Greek word is apostolos, meaning one who is sent. In its primary sense it refers to the twelve disciples commissioned by Jesus, plus Paul, who received a direct commission from the risen Christ (Galatians 1:1, 1 Corinthians 15:8-9). The apostles were eyewitnesses to the resurrection and had a foundational, unrepeatable role in establishing the church.

Ephesians 2:20 describes the church as “built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the chief cornerstone.” That language of foundation suggests a historically specific and completed role. What is not debated is that the qualifications for the original apostolic role – direct witness to the risen Christ, direct commission from him – cannot be replicated.

Key texts: Matthew 10:1-4, Luke 6:12-16, Acts 1:21-26, 1 Corinthians 15:5-9, Galatians 1:1, Ephesians 2:20, Ephesians 4:11

Elder / Overseer / Bishop

This is the primary ongoing leadership role in the New Testament church, and understanding it requires holding two Greek words together.

Presbyteros means elder – someone of recognized maturity and standing in the community. It is the word from which we get “presbyter” and “Presbyterian.”

Episkopos means overseer or bishop – someone who watches over and is responsible for the community’s welfare.

These two words appear to describe the same role from different angles. In Acts 20:17, Paul summons the “elders” of the Ephesian church. In verse 28, he tells those same people: “Keep watch over yourselves and all the flock of which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers.” Titus 1:5-7 moves between the two words in the same sentence.

Three things stand out about this role.

First, elders almost always appear in the plural. “Appoint elders in every town” (Titus 1:5). “The elders who direct the affairs of the church” (1 Timothy 5:17). “Is anyone among you sick? Let them call the elders of the church” (James 5:14). Paul and Barnabas “appointed elders in each church” (Acts 14:23). The New Testament does not describe a single-elder church or a solo senior pastor as its normal model. Plural eldership with shared responsibility is the consistent pattern.

Second, the elder’s function is explicitly pastoral and protective. Acts 20:28-29: “Be shepherds of the church of God… I know that after I leave, savage wolves will come in among you and will not spare the flock.” The elder’s job is to feed and protect the community.

Third, the qualifications are almost entirely about character, not competence or charisma.

Key texts: Acts 14:23, Acts 20:17-28, Philippians 1:1, 1 Timothy 3:1-7, 1 Timothy 5:17-19, Titus 1:5-9, James 5:14, 1 Peter 5:1-4

Deacon

The Greek word diakonos means servant or minister. Acts 6:1-7 describes the apostles asking the community to identify seven people of good standing to handle the practical distribution of food. By the time Paul writes to Philippi, he addresses “overseers and deacons” as recognized roles (Philippians 1:1), and 1 Timothy 3:8-13 lists qualifications for deacons alongside those for overseers. The function is practical service – care for the material needs of the community. Phoebe is described as a deacon of the church at Cenchreae in Romans 16:1.

Key texts: Acts 6:1-7, Philippians 1:1, 1 Timothy 3:8-13, Romans 16:1

Pastor / Shepherd

The Greek word poimen means shepherd. It appears as a specific leadership title only once – Ephesians 4:11 lists “pastors and teachers.” The verbal form appears more frequently, including Jesus’ commission to Peter (“Feed my sheep,” John 21:15-17) and Peter’s instruction to elders to “shepherd the flock of God” (1 Peter 5:2).

In modern American evangelical culture, “the pastor” has become the dominant role – the senior leader, primary preacher, organizational head, and public face of the congregation. That role does not map cleanly onto any single New Testament category. It draws from elder, overseer, and the shepherd metaphor, while adding organizational and platform elements that come from American culture rather than the New Testament.

Key texts: Ephesians 4:11, John 10:11-16, John 21:15-17, 1 Peter 5:2-4, Acts 20:28

Teacher, Prophet, Evangelist

These three appear together in Ephesians 4:11 as gifts Christ gave to the church. Teachers instruct in the faith – James 3:1 attaches particular weight to this: “we who teach will be judged more strictly.” Prophets speak words of encouragement, strengthening, and comfort to the community (1 Corinthians 14:3), and New Testament prophecy is explicitly subject to communal discernment: “the others should weigh carefully what is said” (1 Corinthians 14:29). Evangelists proclaim the gospel to those who have not yet heard it.

Key texts: Ephesians 4:11, 1 Corinthians 12:28-29, 1 Corinthians 14:1-5, 1 Corinthians 14:29, James 3:1, Acts 21:8, 2 Timothy 4:5

The qualifications for leadership

The two most detailed lists of leadership qualifications are in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1. Reading them carefully reveals something important: the qualifications are overwhelmingly about character, not competence, platform, or performance.

1 Timothy 3:1-13 – Overseers and Deacons

Deacons are similarly required to be worthy of respect, sincere, not pursuing dishonest gain, holding the faith with a clear conscience, and above reproach.

Titus 1:5-9 – Elders

Titus overlaps substantially with 1 Timothy 3 but adds: not overbearing, not quick-tempered, loving what is good, upright, holy, and disciplined. It explicitly requires the elder to hold firmly to trustworthy teaching “so that he can encourage others by sound doctrine and refute those who oppose it.”

What the qualifications tell us

The lists have almost nothing to say about preaching ability, organizational skill, visionary leadership, platform presence, charisma, or the capacity to grow a church numerically. Character is the foundation. The one skill explicitly mentioned is the ability to teach.

This is striking given how contemporary churches often select leaders. Charisma, communication ability, vision, and organizational capacity tend to dominate selection processes. The New Testament’s emphasis is almost entirely elsewhere.

The qualifications also function as disqualifying criteria in reverse. A person who is domineering, quick-tempered, financially corrupt, or whose family life is in disorder is not qualified for eldership – regardless of their gifts, their following, or their evident fruitfulness. These are prerequisites, not aspirational targets.

1 Peter 5:1-4 adds three contrasts worth remembering:

Not because you must, but because you are willing. Not pursuing dishonest gain, but eager to serve. Not lording it over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock.

Compulsion versus willingness. Financial exploitation versus eagerness to serve. Domination versus example. These three contrasts map almost exactly onto the patterns of leadership failure the rest of the New Testament describes.

How the New Testament says leadership goes wrong

The New Testament is remarkably specific about leadership failure. It does not treat it as a theoretical risk. It treats it as an expected and recurring reality that communities need to be equipped to recognize and resist.

The wolves Jesus warned about

Matthew 7:15-20 – “Watch out for false prophets. They come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ferocious wolves. By their fruit you will recognize them.”

False leaders do not announce themselves as wolves. They appear as sheep – they use the right language, perform the right roles, appear in the right contexts. The only reliable indicator is fruit. Not gifts. Not anointing. Not platform. Fruit – what their leadership actually produces in people over time.

Acts 20:29-30 – Paul’s farewell to the Ephesian elders: “I know that after I leave, savage wolves will come in among you and will not spare the flock. Even from your own number men will arise and distort the truth in order to draw away disciples after them.”

Two categories: wolves from outside, and distorters from within. The internal threat – leaders who arise from within the community and draw people toward themselves rather than toward the truth – is named as equally real.

Financial corruption

The New Testament returns to money and leadership repeatedly, suggesting this is one of the most consistent failure points.

1 Timothy 6:5-10 – Paul describes people who think “godliness is a means to financial gain” and calls this corruption. “For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil.” Financial integrity appears in every major list of leadership qualifications: not a lover of money, not pursuing dishonest gain (1 Timothy 3:3, 8; Titus 1:7).

2 Peter 2:3 – False teachers “in their greed will exploit you with fabricated stories.”

2 Peter 2:14-15 – They are “experts in greed” who “have left the straight way.”

Titus 1:11 – False teachers are “ruining whole households by teaching things they ought not to teach – and that for the sake of dishonest gain.”

The pattern is consistent: when money becomes a motive for ministry, something fundamental has gone wrong. The New Testament does not treat financial exploitation as an edge case. It treats it as a predictable expression of corrupt character in leadership.

The desire for dominance and control

1 Peter 5:3 explicitly prohibits elders “lording it over those entrusted to you.” The Greek word is katakyrieuo – to exercise dominion over, to rule in a dominating way. It is the same word Jesus uses in Matthew 20:25 when he contrasts Gentile rulers who “lord it over” people with the servant model he is establishing.

3 John 9-10 gives a specific example: Diotrephes, “who loves to be first,” refuses to welcome other leaders, spreads malicious nonsense about them, and expels from the church those who disagree with him. John names this directly and says he will confront it.

2 Corinthians 11:20 – Paul describes people tolerating leaders who “enslave you or exploit you or take advantage of you or put on airs or slap you in the face” – naming what the Corinthians are enduring as the absurdity it is.

The desire for dominance in leadership is not a neutral personality trait. The New Testament consistently frames it as incompatible with genuine Christian leadership.

False teaching and doctrinal corruption

Galatians 1:6-9 – Paul is unambiguous: “Even if we or an angel from heaven should preach a gospel other than the one we preached to you, let them be under God’s curse!” He repeats it for emphasis.

2 Timothy 4:3-4 – “The time will come when people will not put up with sound doctrine. Instead, to suit their own desires, they will gather around them a great number of teachers to say what their itching ears want to hear.”

2 Peter 2:1-3 – “There will be false teachers among you. They will secretly introduce destructive heresies… Many will follow their depraved conduct and will bring the way of truth into disrepute.”

Colossians 2:8 – “See to it that no one takes you captive through hollow and deceptive philosophy, which depends on human tradition and the elemental spiritual forces of this world rather than on Christ.”

Manipulation and exploitation of vulnerable people

2 Corinthians 11:13-15 – “For such people are false apostles, deceitful workers, masquerading as apostles of Christ. And no wonder, for Satan himself masquerades as an angel of light.”

The language of masquerade is important. False leadership does not typically announce itself. It uses the appearance of genuine leadership – the language, the role, the context – while pursuing something different underneath.

2 Timothy 3:6-7 – Paul describes people who “worm their way into homes and gain control over gullible” people “who are loaded down with sins and are swayed by all kinds of evil desires.” The language of gaining control over vulnerable people is explicit.

Hypocrisy

Matthew 23 is Jesus’ most extended criticism of religious leadership. A few lines:

“They do not practice what they preach.” (v. 3)

“Everything they do is done for people to see.” (v. 5)

“They love the place of honor at banquets and the most important seats in the synagogues.” (v. 6)

“You are like whitewashed tombs, which look beautiful on the outside but on the inside are full of the bones of the dead and everything unclean.” (v. 27)

Jesus is describing a consistent pattern: leaders whose public presentation does not match their private reality, who leverage religious authority for social status, and who make the community carry burdens they themselves do not carry.

Silencing those who raise concerns

3 John 9-10 describes Diotrephes expelling from the church those who disagree with him – using membership and belonging as leverage against people who raise legitimate concerns.

Galatians 2:11-14 – Paul publicly opposes Peter “to his face” when Peter’s behavior is “not in line with the truth of the gospel.” This text establishes that even the most recognized leaders are accountable to confrontation when they are wrong – and that such confrontation is an act of faithfulness rather than disloyalty.

Testing leaders and spirits

Given the consistent New Testament awareness that false leadership is real, predictable, and often convincing in appearance, it also provides guidance for discernment. This is not a call to suspicion toward all leaders. It is a call to the kind of clear-eyed evaluation that protects communities and honors genuine leadership.

1 John 4:1-3 – Test the spirits

“Dear friends, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God, because many false prophets have gone out into the world.”

The instruction is direct: do not simply receive spiritual claims at face value. Test them. The principle of testing rather than automatically receiving is foundational.

Matthew 7:15-20 – The fruit test

“By their fruit you will recognize them. Do people pick grapes from thornbushes, or figs from thistles? Likewise, every good tree bears good fruit, but a bad tree bears bad fruit.”

What does this leadership actually produce? Not what does it claim, not what does it look like, not what gifts does it display – what fruit does it bear over time?

This is a patient test. Fruit takes time to appear. But it is reliable, because character eventually expresses itself in what it produces. Some useful fruit questions:

  • Are people in this leader’s care growing toward maturity and independence, or toward greater dependence on the leader?
  • Does this leader’s teaching make people more free or more controlled?
  • When this leader makes a mistake, do they acknowledge it or deflect it?
  • Are people who raise concerns treated with respect or punished?
  • Does the leader’s private life match their public presentation?
  • Are vulnerable people protected or exploited?
  • Does financial transparency exist, or is money handled opaquely?

The two Old Testament tests the New Testament assumes

Deuteronomy 18:21-22 establishes the predictive test: if a prophet speaks in God’s name and the thing does not happen, the Lord did not send them.

But Deuteronomy 13:1-3 establishes something more unsettling: even if a prophet’s sign or wonder comes true, if they then lead people away from God, do not listen to them. A correct prediction or a genuine miracle does not validate the message or the messenger.

The New Testament assumes both of these. Matthew 7:22-23 makes it explicit: “Many will say to me on that day, ‘Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name and in your name drive out demons and in your name perform many miracles?’ Then I will tell them plainly, ‘I never knew you.’” Signs and wonders alone do not authenticate leadership.

1 Thessalonians 5:19-22 – Test everything, hold the good

“Do not quench the Spirit. Do not treat prophecies with contempt but test them all; hold on to what is good, reject every kind of evil.”

The instruction is neither uncritical acceptance nor blanket rejection. Test everything. Hold what is good. Reject what is evil. This requires discernment rather than either naive trust or reflexive suspicion.

Galatians 1:8-9 – The doctrinal standard

“But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach a gospel other than the one we preached to you, let them be under God’s curse!”

The doctrinal test – does this teaching align with the apostolic gospel? – is non-negotiable regardless of the authority or apparent supernatural credentials of the teacher.

2 Timothy 3:1-5 – Character markers to watch for

Paul gives Timothy a list of character markers that signal dangerous leadership: lovers of themselves, lovers of money, boastful, proud, abusive, ungrateful, without love, slanderous, without self-control, brutal, not lovers of the good, treacherous, conceited, lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God – “having a form of godliness but denying its power. Have nothing to do with such people.”

The phrase “having a form of godliness but denying its power” is important. These are not people obviously outside the church. They have the form – the appearance, the language, the context – of godliness. The problem is underneath.

The community’s role in accountability

The New Testament does not describe a model where a single leader is accountable to no one.

1 Timothy 5:19-20 – “Do not entertain an accusation against an elder unless it is brought by two or three witnesses. But those elders who are sinning you are to reprove before everyone, so that the others may take warning.” Accusations require corroboration. But confirmed sin is addressed publicly, not quietly managed.

Matthew 18:15-20 – The process of addressing wrongdoing involves escalating community involvement.

Galatians 6:1 – “Brothers and sisters, if someone is caught in a sin, you who live by the Spirit should restore that person gently.” Restoration is the goal; accountability is the path.

The consistent picture is of a community that takes leadership seriously enough to establish real accountability – not naive deference and not reflexive suspicion, but attentive discernment over time.

Red flags and green flags

One practical way to apply everything we have covered is to look for patterns. No single moment tells the whole story, but patterns over time are revealing. Here are some worth paying attention to.

🚩 Red flag 🟢 Green flag
Leaders work to remove your agency and independent judgment Leaders support you in making your own decisions and growing your own discernment
The leader is the hero of the story God is the hero of the story
A single leader acts alone, unaccountable to others A plurality of independent leaders share authority and hold each other accountable
Mistakes and failures are covered up or denied Issues are addressed openly, honestly, and with care for those affected
One group – a race, nationality, class, or tribe – is taught to be superior or more valued The community actively seeks reconciliation across dividing lines and treats all people as equally made in God’s image
Different perspectives are treated as dissent to be silenced or punished Different perspectives are welcomed as part of how a community grows in understanding
“We have it right” – certainty that the community holds the full and final truth “We are earnestly trying to get it right” – humility about what is still being understood
Financial information is opaque or giving is tied to spiritual standing Financial transparency is normal and giving is free from pressure or manipulation
Loyalty to the leader is expected above loyalty to conscience or truth Loyalty to truth and to people is expected above loyalty to the institution
People who leave are shamed, shunned, or spoken of as spiritually compromised People who leave are treated with dignity and their reasons are taken seriously
Vulnerable people are exposed or used Vulnerable people are protected and centered
The leader’s private life is shielded from any scrutiny The leader’s life is consistent and can withstand honest observation over time

A few plain statements

Before we close, some things worth saying directly.

Not everything done in the name of Jesus is from Jesus.

Not every community that calls itself a church is functioning as one.

Not every person who uses biblical language is teaching biblical truth.

Having genuine gifts does not mean someone has genuine character.

The presence of real ministry does not guarantee the absence of real harm.

The New Testament knows all of this. It is not naive about what gets done in God’s name. And it consistently calls communities to the patient, clear-eyed work of discernment – not to protect themselves from all leaders, but to take leadership seriously enough to evaluate it honestly.

Summary

Category What the NT establishes
Primary ongoing role Elder / Overseer – almost always plural
Servant role Deacon – practical care for community needs
Primary qualifications Character above all; ability to teach
Leadership function Feed, protect, equip, serve – not control
Expected failure modes Financial corruption, dominance, false teaching, hypocrisy, exploitation
Tests for leadership Fruit over time, doctrinal faithfulness, character, community accountability
Community’s role Active discernment, not passive deference

Questions worth sitting with

  1. How does the New Testament picture of leadership – plural elders, character-based qualifications, servant function – compare to what you have experienced in churches you have been part of?

  2. The qualifications for eldership are almost entirely about character rather than competence or charisma. What would it look like for a church to actually select leaders that way?

  3. Jesus says false leaders are known by their fruit, not their appearance or their gifts. Looking back at leaders who have shaped your experience of the church – what fruit did they produce? What did their leadership actually do to people over time?

  4. The New Testament instruction is to test everything, hold what is good, reject what is evil – neither naive trust nor blanket suspicion. Where on that spectrum do you find yourself right now, and what got you there?

  5. Paul publicly opposed Peter when Peter was wrong. The New Testament assumes that even recognized leaders can be confronted and corrected. Have you ever been in a community where that kind of accountability was genuinely possible? What made it possible or impossible?
