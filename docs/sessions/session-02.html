Overview

This session does one main thing: it gives participants an accurate definition of “church” before they spend eight weeks arguing about it.

Most frustration with the church — and most defense of it — is actually a conversation about different things using the same word. This session creates a shared vocabulary.

1. Opening Exercise — What Do We Mean? (0:10–0:25)

Put these five sentences on a screen or whiteboard. Read each one aloud, then ask: “What does the word ‘church’ mean in this sentence?”

Tip
  1. “Where do you go to church?”
  2. “Church starts at 10.”
  3. “We haven’t been to church lately.”
  4. “That church has great preaching.”
  5. “We’re looking for a church with a good youth program.”

Go through them one at a time. Let the group name what “church” is pointing to. They will notice that it means something different in almost every sentence — a place, an event, a habit, an organization, a service provider.

Note

🗒️ Facilitator note: Don’t correct anything yet. Just surface the range. Then say: “So when we say ‘the church has failed people’ or ‘the church changed my life’ — what are we actually talking about? Tonight we try to find out what the New Testament is pointing to when it uses this word.”

2. Teaching — Ekklēsia in the New Testament (0:25–0:55)

Note

🗒️ Facilitator note: Keep each section to 5–8 minutes of talking. Pause for reactions. Never more than 10 consecutive minutes of you speaking. The goal is guided discovery, not lecture.

The word itself

The Greek word behind our English “church” is:

ἐκκλησία — ekklēsia (ek-klay-SEE-ah) Usually translated: church, assembly, congregation

Important

Key point: This word was not invented by Christians. It was ordinary Greek for an assembly of people. When a first-century Christian heard ekklēsia, their mental picture was not a building with pews and programs. It was: a people, an assembly, a gathered community.

There is also an interesting translation wrinkle: our English word “church” does not come from ekklēsia at all. It comes from kyriakon, meaning “belonging to the Lord.”

Language Word Root
English church kyriakon
Scottish kirk kyriakon
German Kirche kyriakon
English (academic) ecclesiastical ekklēsia

Knowing the Greek can correct some of the baggage we unconsciously attach to the English word.

How the New Testament uses ekklēsia

Use Example Meaning
Ordinary crowd Acts 19:32 A pagan mob in confusion
Civic assembly Acts 19:39 Official public gathering
Israel’s assembly Acts 7:38 God’s OT people gathered
House church Romans 16:5 Believers at Priscilla & Aquila’s home
City church 1 Cor. 1:2 All Christians in Corinth
Gathered meeting 1 Cor. 11:18 Christians actually assembled
Universal church Eph. 1:22–23 The whole body of Christ
Tip

Table exercise (5 min): In pairs, read Acts 19:32, 39, and 41. Is Luke saying “the church was in confusion”? What does this tell us about the word ekklēsia?

What Jesus said

Ekklēsia appears only in Matthew, twice:

Matthew 16:18 — “I will build my* ekklēsia.” Matthew 18:17 — “Tell it to the* ekklēsia.”

Two things matter here:

  1. The possessive — Jesus says *“my* ekklēsia.” The church belongs to Christ, not to a denomination, building, or pastor.
  2. The concreteness — In Matthew 18, the ekklēsia is a recognizable community that can hear a dispute and respond. This is not abstract theology. It is an actual group of people who know each other.
Important

The tension: The church is both a theological reality and a specific community of actual people. The New Testament holds both at once.

Acts shows us the people

“Saul began to destroy the church.” (Acts 8:3)

Saul was not destroying buildings. He was entering houses and dragging people away. So what was “the church” that Saul was attacking?

People.

Paul does not write “To Corinth Community Church.” He writes “To God’s ekklēsia that is in Corinth.” That is a subtle but significant difference.

One popular explanation that does not quite work

You have probably heard this:

“Ekklēsia comes from ek (out of) and kaleō (to call), so the church literally means ‘the called-out ones.’“

Do not teach it that way. Acts 19 disproves it — the pagan mob rioting in Ephesus is called an ekklēsia. Luke is not suggesting rioters are spiritually called-out ones. By the NT period, the word simply meant assembly.

✅ Accurate ❌ Not quite right
“God has called his people.” “Ekklēsia literally means called-out ones.”
“Christians are called out of darkness.” (1 Pet. 2:9) “The Greek proves the church is defined by being called out.”

BREAK (0:55–1:05)

3. Teaching — The Temple Connection (1:05–1:35)

The New Testament makes a stunning claim: the people do not go to a sacred place. The people become the sacred place.

The progression under the Old Covenant

God’s presence was associated with a place, structured around restricted access:

Tabernacle → Temple → Holy of Holies → Curtain

That architecture communicated: God is holy, humanity is estranged, access is restricted.

Then at Jesus’ death:

“The curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom.” — Matthew 27:51

Hebrews makes the connection explicit: believers now have direct confidence to enter God’s presence through Christ (Hebrews 10:19–22).

So what happens to the sacred place?

Tip

Small group exercise (8 min): Divide into three groups. Each reads one passage and answers: “What does this say the church is? What would a community that actually believed this look like?“

  • Group 1: 1 Corinthians 3:16–17
  • Group 2: Ephesians 2:19–22
  • Group 3: 1 Peter 2:4–5

Report back.

Important

The New Testament does not replace a Jewish sacred building with a Christian sacred building. It does something far more radical: the people become the building.

God no longer primarily dwells among his people in a sacred structure. He dwells in his people by the Spirit.

Four images for the church

Image Greek Key text What it communicates
Assembly ekklēsia Matt. 16:18 A gathered people belonging to Christ
Body of Christ sōma Christou Eph. 1:22–23 Joined to Christ, joined to each other
Temple of God naos theou 1 Cor. 3:16 God’s Spirit dwells in his people
Household of God oikos theou Eph. 2:19 Family, belonging, shared life

The reversals

Temple logic Church logic
Concentric rings of access Direct access for all through Christ
Priests mediate God’s presence One mediator: Christ alone (1 Tim. 2:5)
Repeated sacrifice One sacrifice, finished (Heb. 10:12)
World moves toward sacred center Gospel moves outward into the world
National — gathered around Israel International — all nations (Matt. 28:19)
Dividing wall: Jew / Gentile Dividing wall destroyed (Eph. 2:14)

The biblical arc

Stage Description
Eden God with humanity
Tabernacle God dwelling among Israel
Temple God’s localized covenant presence
Jesus “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us”
Church God’s Spirit dwelling in his people
New Creation “The dwelling place of God is with man” (Rev. 21:3)
Note

🗒️ Facilitator note: Draw this timeline on a whiteboard slowly. Pause at each step. Ask: “So where does the church fit in this story?” Let them place it before you name it.

The church is an intermediate form appropriate to an intermediate age. We do not yet experience God’s presence as we will in the new creation — but neither are we standing behind the curtain waiting for access.

A challenging implication

Important

If that is right, then the way the church exists is supposed to communicate the gospel.

A church can verbally proclaim the priesthood of all believers while structurally making Christians into spectators. It can proclaim that God dwells in his people while functionally treating a building as the locus of God’s activity. It can proclaim reconciliation while organizing itself around demographic similarity. It can proclaim servanthood while creating celebrity leaders.

The question is not only: what does our church say? It is: what does our structure appear to say?

4. Discussion (1:35–1:50)

  1. Before tonight, when you pictured “the church,” what did you picture? How does the ekklēsia material change that — if at all?

  2. The New Testament says God’s Spirit dwells in his people. What would it mean to take that seriously? What would change about how you think about yourself?

  3. The Temple had a curtain separating ordinary people from God’s presence. The gospel tears that curtain. Where have you experienced churches that put the curtain back up — making access to God feel restricted, conditional, or mediated through a leader?

    This may open difficult territory. Hold it carefully.

  4. Ephesians 2 says the church is supposed to embody reconciliation between people who were previously divided. Where have you seen that happen? Where have you seen churches reinforce those divisions instead?

Close (1:50–2:00)

“The New Testament’s picture of church is simpler and more radical than what most of us have experienced. It’s people — belonging to Christ, joined to each other, becoming the place where God’s presence lives in the world. Everything else we call ‘church’ is downstream from that.

Next week we ask: if that’s the purpose, what does the church actually need to do — and how did we get from people gathered in houses to organizations with staff, buildings, programs, and budgets? That turns out to be a really interesting story.”

Tip

Seed question for the week:

Think about a place or moment where you most clearly sensed God’s presence. Was it in a building? A gathering? A moment with one other person? What does your answer tell you about where you instinctively locate God’s presence — and how does that compare to what the New Testament describes?
