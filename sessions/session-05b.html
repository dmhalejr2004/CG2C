Most people find that the flags are easy to recognize in retrospect and very hard to see in real time. This session is about understanding why — what specific dynamics make certain kinds of leadership failure so hard to name while you are inside them.

We are going to look at three connected things: what narcissism actually is (and what the research shows about it in church leaders), what failure actually looks like and how we categorize it, and then the genuinely hard questions that follow — what to do, who gets restored, and how.

Part 1: Narcissism and church leadership

Where the word comes from

The term comes from the Greek myth of Narcissus — a young man of extraordinary beauty who, upon seeing his own reflection in a pool of water, fell so deeply in love with it that he could not leave. He either wastes away staring at himself or dies trying to embrace the reflection, depending on the version. Ovid gives the most famous account in the Metamorphoses.

Sigmund Freud introduced the clinical use in 1914 in his essay “On Narcissism,” using it to describe an excessive self-love that interferes with the capacity to love others. From there it became a central concept in personality psychology and eventually entered the diagnostic vocabulary.

The name is telling: narcissism is not primarily about self-confidence. It is about being captivated by your own reflection — unable to see others clearly because you are, in some sense, always looking at yourself.

What narcissism actually is

In academic psychology, narcissism is a personality trait — or cluster of traits — that exists on a spectrum. Almost everyone has some degree of narcissistic traits. The question is where someone falls and how those traits interact with their context and role.

Core features include:

  • An inflated sense of self-importance and superiority
  • A strong need for admiration and external validation
  • A sense of entitlement — the belief that special treatment is deserved
  • A tendency to use others to achieve one’s own goals
  • Lack of empathy, or empathy that is instrumental rather than genuine
  • Envy of others alongside a belief that others envy them
  • Arrogance and grandiosity in presentation

Researchers distinguish two forms that look quite different:

Grandiose narcissism is the most visible form — characterized by extraversion, dominance, and a confident, charismatic surface. Grandiose narcissists seek the spotlight, are comfortable with conflict, and project strength and certainty.

Vulnerable narcissism is less visible but equally important — characterized by hypersensitivity to criticism, a fragile sense of self requiring constant protection, and a kind of wounded grandiosity: the belief that one deserves more than one is getting. Vulnerable narcissists can appear humble on the surface while harboring deep convictions of special status and deep resentment of perceived slights.

Both forms show up in church leadership. The vulnerable form is often harder to identify because it does not look like what people expect narcissism to look like.

Narcissistic Personality Disorder — a different category

Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD) is a clinical diagnosis in the DSM-5, estimated to affect roughly 1–6% of the general population, with higher prevalence in men. To meet the threshold, a person must show a pervasive pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration, and lack of empathy beginning by early adulthood and present across contexts.

Two reasons the distinction between narcissistic traits and NPD matters:

  1. It is not clinically or ethically appropriate to diagnose someone from a distance — including a pastor — with a personality disorder.
  2. Significant harm can be caused by people who have strong narcissistic traits but do not meet the full clinical threshold for NPD. The diagnosis is not required for the damage to be real.

What the research actually shows about pastors

There is a growing body of empirical research on narcissism in pastoral populations. Here is an honest summary of what we know — and how confident we should be.

The central finding: narcissistic traits are present among pastors and can have significant consequences, but we do not yet have strong evidence that pastors, as a population, are more narcissistic than other professionals.

Key studies:

Zondag (2004) — “Just Like Other People: Narcissism Among Pastors” (Pastoral Psychology) — Surveyed Dutch pastors and identified four narcissistic styles: Balanced, Vulnerable, Undisguised, and Masking. The study’s most important contribution is challenging the assumption that narcissism always manifests as obvious arrogance. A pastor might exhibit narcissistic characteristics through insecurity and a strong need for approval rather than overt grandiosity.

Cooper, Pullig, and Dickens (2016) (Journal of Psychology and Theology) — 488 Protestant ministers evaluated ethical scenarios. Higher narcissism was associated with poorer ethical judgment. Importantly, intrinsic religiosity — faith internalized as genuine personal commitment — moderated the negative relationship. This suggests narcissistic tendencies and sincere religious faith can coexist, and that religiosity is not a single, uniform protective factor.

Zondag (2007) — Narcissism and Empathy Among Pastors (Journal of Pastoral Care & Counseling) — Examined how different narcissistic tendencies relate to the capacity for empathy — perspective-taking, empathic concern, and the ability to imagine another’s experience. The implication: we should distinguish between a pastor’s public interpersonal skills and the underlying motivations shaping their relationships. These are not the same thing.

Klemashevich and Nave (2023) (Journal of Psychology and Christianity) — Seminary graduates showed higher-than-average narcissism scores. They also reported high spiritual maturity. Overall narcissism scores were not significantly associated with spiritual maturity measures. This raises an uncomfortable possibility: religious education and perceived spiritual maturity do not necessarily correspond to lower narcissism.

Dunaetz, Jung, and Lambert (2018) (Great Commission Research Journal) — Surveyed 64 Christians across church sizes. Found that participants from larger churches demonstrated significantly greater tolerance for narcissistic leadership than those from smaller churches. The study was small and measured congregant tolerance rather than pastor narcissism — but the finding is suggestive.

How strong is the evidence?

It is worth being honest about what the research does and does not establish:

Research question Strength of evidence
Do pastors exhibit narcissistic traits? Strong — clearly documented
Is narcissism associated with poorer ethical judgment among pastors? Moderate — supported by a substantial empirical study
Are pastors more narcissistic than the general population? Weak — no definitive representative comparison
Do larger churches tolerate narcissistic behavior more? Preliminary — one small study supports the hypothesis
Is narcissism among pastors increasing? Insufficient — no convincing longitudinal evidence

A 2024 meta-analysis of over 546,000 participants spanning 1982–2023 found that narcissism scores in the general population generally decreased rather than increased across that period. This does not rule out a different pattern among pastors, but it should make us cautious about assuming a broader cultural epidemic explains what we are seeing in churches.

Why the church context amplifies the risks

Even if pastoral populations are not more narcissistic to start, the church environment may amplify the consequences of narcissistic traits in specific ways.

The structure of worship creates a built-in admiration supply. A pastor stands at the front every week, receives attention and deference, is thanked and affirmed, and has their words treated as spiritually significant. For someone with high narcissistic traits, this is a powerfully reinforcing environment — and unlike most other leadership contexts, very little structural correction is built in.

Theological language provides cover. The language of calling, anointing, divine vision, and spiritual authority can be used — consciously or not — to place a leader beyond normal accountability. “God told me” is very difficult to argue with. “The Lord has laid this on my heart” forecloses disagreement in ways that “I think we should” does not.

Success is interpreted as divine endorsement. When a church grows, this is often read — by the leader and the community — as evidence of God’s blessing. Growth becomes theological proof. This makes it very difficult to raise concerns about leadership behavior, because the response is: look at what God is doing here.

The selection problem. General leadership research shows that narcissism predicts emergent leadership — who rises — but not effective leadership — who should stay. The characteristics that help someone appear visionary and compelling in the early stages are not the same characteristics that make someone a good shepherd over time. Churches that select primarily for charisma, public confidence, and growth metrics may be selecting for a personality profile that carries long-term risks.

What narcissistic leadership looks like over time

The early stages often feel electric. A narcissistic leader can be genuinely inspiring, energizing, and vision-casting. People feel part of something significant.

Over time, characteristic patterns tend to emerge:

  • The inner circle shrinks to people who offer uncritical loyalty
  • Critics and questioners are marginalized, pushed out, or publicly shamed
  • The leader’s needs — financial, emotional, logistical — become increasingly central to the community’s life
  • Accountability structures are weakened or eliminated
  • The leader’s vision becomes indistinguishable from God’s will in community language
  • People who leave are described in negative spiritual terms
  • Empathy is performative — present on stage, absent in private

Part 2: When leadership fails — naming what happened

A framework for understanding failure

Not every mistake a leader makes is a failure of the same kind. And the response should be proportionate to the category. The following framework is not about assigning blame — it is about being honest enough to name what actually happened.

Category What it is Examples Appropriate response
Imperfection Ordinary human error; mistakes made in good faith and acknowledged Poor decision under pressure; miscommunication; judgment call that didn’t work Honest conversation; feedback; expectation of acknowledgment and growth
Ongoing pattern Repeated behaviors that reveal character rather than circumstance Consistent use of anger to silence people; chronic dishonesty in small things; repeated boundary violations Formal accountability; named pattern; concrete expectations with consequences; time-limited monitoring
Moral failure A serious violation — often of sexual ethics, financial integrity, or truthfulness — that represents a breach of NT qualifications for leadership Sexual affair; financial fraud; sustained deception of the congregation Outside accountability; transparent process with the board; care for those harmed; public acknowledgment
Spiritual abuse The misuse of spiritual authority to control, harm, or silence people Using Scripture to shame or threaten; claiming divine authority to override someone’s choices; punishing those who raise concerns Outside investigation; removal from leadership; professional care for those harmed; potentially civil authorities
Criminal conduct Behavior that violates the law, especially involving vulnerable people Sexual abuse of minors; assault; financial crimes Civil authorities are primary; church does not handle this internally

Church hurt vs. abuse — a distinction that matters

These terms get used interchangeably, and they should not be.

Church hurt is the pain that comes from any of the above — imperfect communities, poor decisions, moral failures, genuine disagreements. It is real, it is significant, and it deserves to be taken seriously. Almost everyone who has been part of a church for long has experienced it in some form.

Abuse is a specific category of harm where a power differential is exploited to control or damage someone. It may be sexual, spiritual, emotional, financial, or physical. It is not simply doing wrong — it is doing wrong to someone in a way that uses power against them.

Church hurt Abuse
Power dynamic May or may not involve a power differential Always involves exploitation of a power differential
Intent May be unintentional May be intentional or patterned — intent doesn’t determine harm
Appropriate response Varies widely; often internal to the community Requires outside accountability; civil authorities where laws broken
Self-awareness of harm Leader may recognize harm done Leader often denies, minimizes, or re-frames harm
Recurrence May be a one-time failure Often a pattern

Not all church hurt is abuse. All abuse causes church hurt. The distinction matters because the appropriate response is fundamentally different.

Why leadership failure hits differently in a church context

When leadership fails in a church, the impact tends to be disproportionately large. This is not a sign of weakness or oversensitivity. There are structural reasons for it.

The trust involved is unusually high. People come to churches in moments of genuine vulnerability — grief, crisis, searching, transition. They extend a level of trust to church leaders that they would not extend in most other contexts. When that trust is violated, the wound is deep precisely because the investment was real.

The spiritual dimensions compound the harm. When a leader is understood as representing God — speaking for God, carrying God’s authority — their failure raises questions that go beyond the relational. Did God fail? Was my faith misplaced? Is the whole framework wrong? The harm is not just relational but theological.

The community often becomes an extension of the harm. In many cases of leadership failure, the congregation or its leadership structure responds in ways that compound the original harm — denying it happened, minimizing it, prioritizing the institution’s reputation, or pressuring victims to be silent for the sake of unity.

The social cost of speaking is enormous. Church communities can be very small social worlds. When someone has been harmed and the community rallies around the leader, the victim can find themselves suddenly without their entire support network. Speaking carries the cost of losing everything.

Part 3: The hard questions

What do you do about it?

The response to leadership failure should be proportionate to the category from the framework above.

For imperfection: Honest feedback, expectation of acknowledgment, time to demonstrate growth.

For ongoing patterns: Formal accountability — conversations with a board, elder team, or denominational structure. Clear naming of what is being observed. Concrete expectations with consequences.

For moral failure: This depends significantly on whether harm to others is involved. The New Testament’s framework in 1 Timothy 5:19-20 is relevant: accusations require corroboration, but confirmed sin is addressed publicly, not privately managed. Outside accountability is usually necessary.

For abuse and criminal conduct: The response is not primarily a church matter. Abuse requires outside accountability — civil authorities where laws have been broken, professional counseling for those harmed, and in many cases legal reporting obligations. The church does not have the expertise, objectivity, or authority to adjudicate abuse internally. Attempts to do so almost always cause further harm.

What do you do when there is no recognition?

This is one of the hardest scenarios: a leader who has caused real harm but does not acknowledge it, and a community that continues to rally around them — often because the church is growing, the leader remains compelling, and the costs of acknowledging the problem feel too high.

Growth is not evidence of God’s blessing on the leader’s character. Churches can grow for many reasons, including the natural human attraction to confident, charismatic leaders — which, as we saw, is not the same thing as effective or healthy leadership. The New Testament’s fruit test is not about attendance. It is about what is being produced in people’s lives over time.

The community’s loyalty may be protecting harm. People who are deeply formed by a community and a leader will often go to significant lengths to explain away evidence of failure. This is not stupidity or weakness. It is the predictable result of strong social bonds, significant personal investment, and the genuine good that has also been present. Recognizing this can reduce the self-blame many harm survivors experience when they ask themselves why they didn’t see it sooner.

You may not be able to fix it. You cannot force a leader to acknowledge failure. You cannot force a community to recognize harm it is not ready to see. What you can do is name what you have experienced clearly, seek support outside the community, and make your own decisions about participation and continued relationship.

External accountability is often the only path. Denominational structures, outside investigators, civil authorities, journalism, and public accountability have historically been the primary mechanisms that produce recognition in church contexts where internal accountability has failed. If internal paths have been exhausted, external ones are legitimate.

What do you do when people you love keep following a failed leader?

People rarely leave a community or a leader because someone told them to. Change tends to happen through their own accumulating experience over time. Your job is not to rescue people from their choices — that approach tends to push people closer to the community rather than toward the door.

What you can do: stay in honest relationship without issuing ultimatums. Ask curious questions rather than making accusations. Be a presence of honest friendship that does not require agreement.

And you can grieve the reality that some people you love may choose differently than you would. That grief is real and it deserves space.

Restoration — the question that divides people

The New Testament’s posture toward people who fail is consistently oriented toward restoration. Galatians 6:1 — restore that person gently. 2 Corinthians 2 — Paul urges the Corinthians to reaffirm love for a person who has been disciplined. The goal is not punishment. It is restoration.

But restoration needs to be understood carefully, because the word is often used to short-circuit the process.

Restoration to the community is different from restoration to leadership. This is probably the most important distinction in this session.

Restoration to community Restoration to leadership
What it means Full membership, belonging, love, and participation in the life of the church Return to a position of authority over others, especially vulnerable people
Who it involves The person and the community The person, the community, and all those who would be placed under their authority
What it requires Genuine acknowledgment of harm; demonstrated change over time Genuine acknowledgment; sustained change over years; rebuilt trust with the harmed; demonstrated fitness to hold power accountable
Who decides The community together The community, but with significant weight given to those who were harmed
Default assumption Restoration to community is the goal and should be pursued Restoration to leadership is not guaranteed; it must be earned and may not be appropriate

Restoration requires genuine acknowledgment. You cannot restore what has not been named. A leader who has not genuinely acknowledged what they did, demonstrated understanding of how it harmed others, and shown sustained change has not done the work. Restoration is not a process that happens because enough time has passed. It happens because something has genuinely changed.

Restoration takes time. Any process that restores a leader to a platform within months of a disclosed failure should be viewed with significant skepticism. Genuine character change, rebuilding of trust, and demonstrated transformation take years, not months.

Some failures are permanently disqualifying from leadership

This is the hardest thing in this section to say, and it needs to be said.

The New Testament’s qualifications for elders and overseers include being “above reproach” and having “a good reputation with outsiders” (1 Timothy 3). Some failures so thoroughly violate these qualifications that restoration to a position of authority over vulnerable people is not appropriate, regardless of personal repentance.

This is not about punishment. It is about protection. Some categories of failure are permanently disqualifying from leadership because:

  • The trust required for pastoral authority cannot be meaningfully rebuilt
  • The risk to vulnerable people who would be placed under that authority is too high
  • The character failure demonstrated was not situational but dispositional

Categories that are almost certainly permanently disqualifying from pastoral leadership:

  • Sexual abuse of minors
  • Sexual abuse or coercion of adult congregants or ministry workers
  • Sustained financial fraud against the congregation
  • A documented pattern of spiritual abuse — systematic use of religious authority to harm
  • Conviction for violent crime

Repentance and restoration to community are possible and should be pursued. Restoration to leadership is a separate question — and the answer is sometimes no.

The fact that someone has genuinely changed does not automatically mean they should be trusted with power over vulnerable people again. We do not require that a disbarred attorney be reinstated to show that their repentance is genuine. The same logic applies here.

The thread connecting everything

Narcissism and leadership failure are not unrelated topics. They are two expressions of the same underlying dynamic: the confusion of the leader’s self with the mission, the community, and God.

When a narcissistic leader enters a church, the leader’s needs gradually become indistinguishable from the church’s needs. When failure happens and is not acknowledged, the leader’s reputation becomes indistinguishable from the community’s identity. When restoration is mishandled, the leader’s comfort becomes indistinguishable from the community’s healing.

The New Testament’s consistent counter to all of this is the same: plural leadership, communal discernment, the fruit test applied over time, and the willingness to confront what is true even when it is costly.

None of that is easy. All of it is necessary.
