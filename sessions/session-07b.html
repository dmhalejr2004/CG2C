Picking up the thread

The last two sessions named things. Red flags and green flags. Narcissism and what it looks like in a leader. The spectrum from disagreement to hurt to abuse. Why betrayal by a spiritual authority cuts so deep.

Naming things matters. But naming is not the same as knowing what to do next.

That is where most people get stuck. They know something happened. They may even know what category it falls into. But the next question – what do I do with it? – tends to produce a tangle of competing impulses: be gracious, hold people accountable, protect others, forgive, don’t be bitter, seek justice, move on, don’t cover things up. These impulses feel like they contradict each other, and sometimes people get so tangled in the tension that they do nothing at all.

This session tries to untangle that. It starts by separating concepts that get fused together when they should stay distinct. Then it moves to the practical question of what a responsible response actually looks like, step by step. And it closes with a preview of something we need to understand before the final session: the theological frameworks that have been quietly rewiring how large portions of the American church understand authority, prophecy, and power.

Part 1: Four things that are not the same

The confusion in most conversations about leadership failure comes from collapsing four distinct concepts into one or two. Once you can hold them separately, the false choices mostly dissolve.

Forgiveness

Forgiveness is a decision the harmed person makes about their own interior life. It is the choice to release the debt – not to pretend it was not owed, not to declare the harm unreal, not automatically to restore the relationship. It is the decision to stop organizing your inner life around what you are owed.

Theologically, forgiveness is grounded in what God has done: “Be kind and compassionate to one another, forgiving each other, just as in Christ God forgave you” (Ephesians 4:32). But notice what that passage does not say. It does not say that forgiveness removes consequences, or that forgiveness requires restored access, or that forgiveness means pretending something did not happen.

Forgiveness is primarily an act of release that benefits the one forgiving. Psychologically, sustained unforgiveness keeps a person bound to the harm. Spiritually, the New Testament treats it as a posture the follower of Jesus moves toward. But forgiveness is interior. It lives in the person who was harmed. No one else can demand it on a timeline, and it does not automatically produce any external outcome.

Restoration

Restoration is an external reality. It describes a relationship or a status being rebuilt after it was broken. The New Testament does speak of restoration: “Brothers and sisters, if someone is caught in a sin, you who live by the Spirit should restore that person gently” (Galatians 6:1).

But restoration has two very different forms, and conflating them is one of the most common mistakes in how churches handle leadership failure.

Restoration to community means full belonging, love, participation, and membership. Someone is not permanently defined by their worst failure. The community does not excommunicate people who have genuinely failed and genuinely repented. This is always the goal, and it reflects the actual orientation of the New Testament.

Restoration to leadership means returning to a position of authority over other people – particularly over vulnerable people who extend trust. This is a completely separate question. It is not automatic. It is not guaranteed by repentance. It is not owed to anyone. It must be evaluated on its own terms, with the primary consideration being the safety and wellbeing of the people who would be placed under that authority.

A person can be fully restored to community – welcomed, loved, included – while remaining permanently removed from a leadership role. These are not in tension. They operate in different categories.

Justice

Justice is a social and structural reality. It is about what is owed at the level of the community, the institution, and sometimes the state. Justice asks: has the wrong been named? Have those harmed been acknowledged? Have the systems that allowed or enabled the harm been changed? Has accountability been real, or only performed?

Justice is not primarily about what happens to the offender. It is about whether the community has righted what was wrong – whether truth has been told, whether those harmed have been seen, whether the conditions that produced the harm have been addressed.

The Old Testament concept of justice (mishpat) is relational and restorative: it is about putting things right in a community, not simply punishing individuals. The prophets are relentless about this. Isaiah 1:17 – “Learn to do right; seek justice. Defend the oppressed. Take up the cause of the fatherless; plead the case of the widow.” Justice is what it looks like when a community actually responds to harm in a way that honors those who were hurt.

Consequences

Consequences are what happen in the external world as a result of someone’s choices. They exist independently of forgiveness, independently of restoration, and independently of whether the offending person has repented.

A person who has driven drunk and caused an accident faces consequences regardless of whether the other driver forgives them. A surgeon who operates while impaired faces licensing consequences regardless of whether their patient forgives them. A pastor who has abused a congregant faces consequences – removal from ministry, civil liability, possible criminal accountability – regardless of whether they have repented and been forgiven.

Consequences are not punitive in the sense of being arbitrary punishment. They are the natural and appropriate result of choices made, designed to protect others from similar harm in the future.

Four concepts – four different domains

Concept Domain Who decides What it requires What it does not require
Forgiveness Interior / personal The person harmed A personal decision to release the debt Restored relationship; removed consequences; repentance by the offender
Restoration to community Relational The community Genuine acknowledgment; demonstrated change Return to leadership; immediate trust
Restoration to leadership Institutional The community with significant weight to those harmed Years of demonstrated change; rebuilt trust; safety for those who would be in their care Forgiveness alone; repentance alone
Justice Social / structural The community and its structures Truth told; harm acknowledged; conditions changed That the offender suffer; that the harmed person forgive
Consequences External / legal The situation, the institution, civil authorities Whatever the harm and its context determine Unforgiveness; bitterness; punitive intent

Part 2: The false dichotomies

Once you can hold those four concepts separately, the most common false choices become visible as the category errors they are.

“You can have mercy or justice, but not both.”

This assumes mercy and justice operate in the same domain and therefore cancel each other out. They do not. Mercy is what you extend relationally – you do not treat someone with contempt, you do not use their failure as a weapon against them, you hold open the possibility of restoration. Justice is what you pursue structurally – you name the harm, you change the conditions, you ensure accountability. A community can be genuinely merciful toward a person who has failed and simultaneously insist on structural accountability. These are not competing options.

“If you really forgive, you will drop the consequences.”

This is probably the most damaging of the false choices, because it gets used regularly to pressure harm survivors into silence. The claim is that genuine forgiveness requires the removal of consequences. But forgiveness and consequences operate in entirely separate domains. Interior release (forgiveness) has no logical bearing on external accountability (consequences). A person can genuinely, fully, sincerely forgive someone and simultaneously report them to civil authorities, refuse to allow them to return to ministry, or testify against them in a legal proceeding. These are not contradictions. They are decisions made in different categories.

“Pursuing accountability means you haven’t forgiven.”

This one is especially insidious because it weaponizes a virtue to silence accountability. It reframes the person who is pursuing accountability as the problem – as someone who is bitter, unforgiving, unChristian. But accountability is not the absence of forgiveness. It is the appropriate structural response to harm. The two can coexist completely.

“Grace means giving people another chance.”

Grace is real and it means something. But grace does not mean that every position of power must eventually be restored to every person who has abused it. Giving a repentant embezzler another chance at relationship does not require giving them access to the church’s bank accounts. Giving a repentant pastor who has abused congregants another chance at community does not require giving them another pulpit. Grace operates in the domain of relationship and belonging. Access to vulnerable people is a separate category with its own criteria.

Part 3: A framework for disqualification from ministry

The New Testament is explicit that leadership in the church carries a higher standard. James 3:1 – “Not many of you should become teachers, my fellow believers, because you know that we who teach will be judged more strictly.” The qualifications in 1 Timothy 3:1-7 and Titus 1:6-9 describe a character profile that is prerequisite, not aspirational.

The key phrase in both passages is “above reproach” (Greek: anepilemptos in 1 Timothy, anegkletos in Titus). This means: not open to legitimate accusation. Not someone whose life gives grounds for serious criticism. The standard is about the current and ongoing state of a person’s character and conduct, not about whether they have ever failed.

This creates a nuanced framework for thinking about disqualification. Not all failures are the same, and not all disqualification is permanent.

Category 1: Pre-conversion conduct

The New Testament’s framing of conversion is radical: “If anyone is in Christ, the new creation has come: the old has gone, the new is here” (2 Corinthians 5:17). Paul himself was a violent persecutor of Christians before his conversion, and he became perhaps the most significant leader in the early church. The New Testament does not build a system where pre-conversion conduct permanently bars someone from leadership.

What matters is whether the person’s life now – their character, relationships, track record, and reputation – meets the standards of 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1. If it does, pre-conversion history is not disqualifying.

The complicating question is patterns. If conduct that preceded conversion reflects a deep character pattern – particularly around the use of power, deception, or the exploitation of others – and that pattern has continued in some form after conversion, then the issue is not the pre-conversion conduct. The issue is an ongoing pattern that the conversion experience did not address.

Category 2: Post-conversion general failure

Leaders are human. They will fail in ordinary human ways – pride, impatience, poor judgment, relational failure, moral stumbling. The qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 describe what a person’s life should look like in an ongoing way, not a perfect past.

A leader who fails, is genuinely accountable for the failure, demonstrates real change, and rebuilds trust over time may well remain qualified for leadership – or may become qualified again after a period of accountability and restoration. This is the kind of failure the church should have structures to handle. It is not automatically disqualifying. The key questions are: Is the person genuinely accountable? Is there real change? Is trust rebuilt on actual evidence rather than the leader’s own declaration of readiness?

Category 3: Abuse of the office itself

This category is different in kind, not just degree.

When someone uses spiritual authority to harm people – sexual misconduct with congregants or staff, financial fraud against the congregation, systematic manipulation or coercion under the guise of pastoral care, spiritual abuse that exploits the trust relationship – the failure is not incidental to the role. The role is what made the harm possible. The authority is what was weaponized.

This matters enormously for the question of restoration to leadership, for one specific reason: if the person returns to a position of spiritual authority, they are returned to the exact conditions that enabled the harm. They will again be in a position where people extend them unusual trust. They will again have access to vulnerable people in moments of spiritual openness. They will again have authority that is hard to question because it claims divine sanction.

The question is not whether the person has repented. The question is whether people placed under their future authority would be safe.

A third category also belongs here: conduct that violates civil law in ways that are directly entangled with the ministry role. A pastor convicted of financial fraud against the congregation cannot be trusted with the congregation’s finances. A pastor who has sexually abused a congregant cannot be trusted with pastoral relationships. The crime and the role are not separable.

A framework for disqualification

Category Examples Automatically disqualifying? Path to leadership
Pre-conversion conduct Criminal history, moral failure before faith No, unless patterns continue Current character meeting 1 Tim 3 / Titus 1 qualifications
Post-conversion general failure Pride, poor judgment, relational failure, moral stumbling No Genuine accountability, demonstrated change, rebuilt trust
Abuse of the office itself Sexual misconduct with congregants, financial fraud against church, systematic spiritual abuse Likely permanent Extremely high bar; safety of future congregants is primary consideration; not owed to the leader

Part 4: When to involve civil authorities

This is the question people most often avoid, partly because it feels like escalation and partly because of a specific biblical passage that gets applied in ways it was not intended.

What 1 Corinthians 6 actually says

Paul writes to the Corinthian church: “If any of you has a dispute with another, do you dare to take it before the ungodly for judgment instead of before the Lord’s people? … Therefore, if you have disputes about such matters, do you ask for a ruling from those whose way of life is scorned in the church? I say this to shame you. Is it possible that there is nobody among you wise enough to judge a dispute between believers?” (1 Corinthians 6:1-5)

This passage gets used to argue that Christians should never involve civil authorities in church matters. But that application misreads what Paul is actually addressing.

What Paul is addressing: Internal disputes between members of the congregation – the Greek word used is pragmata, meaning business or civil matters, the kind of dispute that in Corinth would have gone to a civil magistrate for arbitration. Paul is frustrated that people with ordinary disagreements are taking each other to public courts rather than handling them internally, in a way that publicly shames the community.

What Paul is not addressing: Crimes against persons. Sexual assault. Financial fraud. The exploitation of vulnerable people. Conduct that falls outside the category of “disputes between believers” and into the category of harm that civil society has an independent interest in addressing.

The limits of internal authority: A church has no subpoena power. It has no investigative capacity. It has no ability to compel testimony, gather forensic evidence, or impose consequences beyond membership. When a harm rises to the level of a crime – particularly crimes involving minors, sexual assault, or financial fraud – the internal church process is structurally incapable of providing the accountability the situation requires.

The legal obligation: In many jurisdictions, clergy are mandated reporters. This is not optional. A legal obligation to report suspected abuse of a minor exists independently of any church process or any desire to handle things internally. Failure to report is itself a crime.

The practical reality: Historically, the primary mechanism that has produced real accountability in cases of serious church misconduct has been external – civil authorities, investigative journalism, denominational oversight, survivor communities. Internal church processes, left alone, have an overwhelming track record of prioritizing institutional reputation over the needs of those harmed.

When to involve civil authorities

Situation Internal process Civil authorities
Disagreement about church direction Yes No
Personal conflict between members Yes Only if attempts fail and legal rights are at stake
Financial misconduct Yes, but with outside accounting Yes, if fraud is involved
Sexual misconduct between adults Yes, with outside support for harmed person Encouraged; required if coercion or assault
Sexual misconduct involving minors Yes, but secondary Required; mandatory reporting laws apply
Physical violence Yes Yes, immediately
Ongoing pattern of spiritual abuse Yes, with outside support Possibly, depending on jurisdiction and conduct

Part 5: Pulling it together – a practical guide

So what do you actually do?

Step 1: Name what you are dealing with

Before deciding what to do, return to the framework from Session 5: is this a disagreement about methods, a disagreement about principles, church hurt, or church abuse? The category shapes the appropriate response. The error of treating disagreement as abuse or treating abuse as disagreement does real harm in both directions.

Step 2: Go directly first, when it is safe to do so

Matthew 18:15-17 describes a process of direct engagement before escalation: “If your brother or sister sins, go and point out their fault, just between the two of you. If they listen to you, you have won them over. But if they will not listen, take one or two others along…”

The principle behind this is sound: deal with things as directly and locally as possible first. This respects the person you are concerned about. It gives them the opportunity to respond before the concern widens. It reflects a genuine commitment to resolution rather than simply to being right.

But the process assumes rough equality of standing. It was not written to instruct someone who has been abused to go alone to confront their abuser. It was not written for situations where direct engagement would endanger the person raising the concern, or where previous attempts at direct conversation have been punished. The principle is valuable. The specific mechanics need contextual judgment.

Step 3: Bring witnesses when direct engagement fails or is inappropriate

The same passage in Matthew 18 describes the next step: bring one or two others. This serves several purposes simultaneously. It provides corroboration. It demonstrates that the concern is serious enough to persist. It introduces accountability for both parties. And it creates a record.

1 Timothy 5:19 echoes this: “Do not entertain an accusation against an elder unless it is brought by two or three witnesses.” Concerns about leaders need corroboration. At the same time, the verse continues: “But those elders who are sinning you are to reprove before everyone, so that the others may take warning.” Confirmed failure is addressed publicly, not managed privately to protect the institution.

Step 4: Involve church leadership or governing structures

If the concern is not addressed after direct engagement and the involvement of witnesses, the matter should go to whatever governing structure the church has – an elder board, deacons, denomination leadership. This step matters even when it feels futile, for two reasons: it creates a record, and it gives the structure an opportunity to respond before the situation moves outside the church.

A realistic note: governing structures often protect the institution rather than those who have been harmed. This is predictable. It does not mean the step should be skipped. It means the step should be taken without naive confidence that it will produce the needed result.

Step 5: Know when to go outside

External accountability becomes appropriate – and sometimes necessary – when:

  • Internal processes have been exhausted without genuine response
  • The conduct involves civil crimes
  • The person in authority is also the governing structure (a sole senior pastor with no accountability above them)
  • The harm involves minors
  • Continuing inside the internal process would endanger someone
  • The institution is actively suppressing the concern

External paths include denominational oversight (when a denomination exists), civil authorities (when the conduct rises to that level), professional counselors and advocates for those harmed, investigative journalists (in cases of significant public harm), and survivor communities.

What to do when nothing works

Sometimes nothing works. The leader does not acknowledge the concern. The governing structure protects itself. External paths produce no results. The community stays intact around the person who caused harm.

This is a real outcome and it happens regularly. It needs to be named honestly, because false hope that “eventually accountability will come” keeps people in damaging situations longer than is good for them.

What you can control: your own choices about what you participate in and what you put your name next to. The accuracy and honesty of your own account of what happened. Whether you are connected to support – people, professionals, communities – who can help you make sense of what happened without requiring the institution to validate your experience.

What you cannot control: other people’s choices. Whether the community wakes up. Whether the leader is ever genuinely accountable. Whether you get the acknowledgment you need and deserve.

Releasing what you cannot control is not giving up. It is protecting yourself from the additional harm of organizing your life around an institution that has demonstrated it will not protect you.

Part 6: A pivot – movements reshaping the church

The last two sessions of this class are going to shift terrain. We have been talking about leadership failure in individual churches – the patterns, the dynamics, the response. Now we need to zoom out and talk about something that has been shaping the broader landscape of American Christianity in ways that matter for everything we have discussed.

There are theological and institutional movements – some explicitly organized, others more diffuse – that have been influencing how large segments of the American church understand authority, prophecy, blessing, and power. Understanding them matters because they create specific conditions that make some of the problems we have been discussing more likely, more intense, and harder to address.

This session introduces one of the foundational elements of several of these movements: the question of prophecy. Session 8 will go deeper into the movements themselves – dominion theology and the Seven Mountain Mandate, the New Apostolic Reformation, prosperity theology, and their entanglement with leadership and authority.

Prophecy: gift, role, and exploitation

What the New Testament actually says

The New Testament describes prophecy as a spiritual gift given to the church for specific purposes. The key passages:

1 Corinthians 14:1-5 – “Follow the way of love and eagerly desire gifts of the Spirit, especially prophecy… the one who prophesies speaks to people for their strengthening, encouraging and comfort… the one who prophesies edifies the church.”

Paul’s framing here is striking for what it emphasizes. Prophecy in this passage is not primarily predictive. It is not primarily about special access to hidden knowledge. It is explicitly oriented toward the community: strengthening, encouragement, comfort, edification. The measure of whether something functions as prophecy, in this framework, is whether it builds up the community.

1 Corinthians 14:29-33 – “Two or three prophets should speak, and the others should weigh carefully what is said… For you can all prophesy in turn so that everyone may be instructed and encouraged. The spirits of prophets are subject to prophets. For God is not a God of disorder but of peace.”

Several things are compressed into these verses: prophecy is plural (multiple voices, not one); it is subject to communal evaluation (“the others should weigh carefully”); the prophet’s spirit is “subject to” the prophet, meaning the experience does not override the prophet’s self-control; and the goal is order, instruction, and encouragement, not drama or hierarchy.

Ephesians 4:11-13 – “So Christ himself gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the pastors and teachers, to equip his people for works of service, so that the body of Christ may be built up until we all reach unity in the faith and in the knowledge of the Son of God and become mature…”

This is a passage we will return to in depth in Session 8, because it is at the center of significant debate about whether the offices of apostle and prophet are still active in the church today. For now, notice the purpose Paul assigns to all of these roles: equipping others, building up the body, moving toward unity and maturity.

1 Thessalonians 5:19-21 – “Do not quench the Spirit. Do not treat prophecies with contempt but test them all; hold on to what is good.”

The posture here is neither credulous nor dismissive. Take prophecy seriously. Test it. Keep what is good.

The gift vs. the role

This is a distinction that matters for understanding the movements we will examine in Session 8.

The gift of prophecy as Paul describes it in 1 Corinthians is accessible across the community – “you can all prophesy in turn” – and is oriented toward encouraging and building up. It is plural, subject to communal evaluation, and not hierarchically organized. It is a spiritual capacity that can function through many different people.

The office or role of prophet is a different claim. It is the assertion that a specific person carries a permanent, authoritative position as a prophet – someone whose words carry authority by virtue of their office, not merely by virtue of community testing and affirmation. This is the kind of claim that some movements have been making with increasing intensity, and it functions very differently from the gift Paul describes in 1 Corinthians.

Gift vs. role: key differences

Gift of prophecy Office/role of prophet
Who has it Accessible across the community Claimed by specific individuals
Authority basis Community testing and affirmation The person’s declared or recognized office
Subject to evaluation Explicitly yes (1 Cor 14:29) Often claimed to be above ordinary evaluation
Purpose Strengthening, encouragement, comfort, edification Varies; often includes governance and direction
Accountability Communal Often claimed to be vertical (accountable to God, not people)
New Testament emphasis Plural, ordered, subject to self-control Debated; Ephesians 4:11 is the primary text

How prophecy gets weaponized

Understanding the difference between the gift and the claimed role is essential for seeing how prophecy becomes a tool of control.

Prophecy as a right to leadership. When a person claims the office of prophet – particularly in traditions that have revived apostolic and prophetic offices – the claim itself becomes a source of authority that is difficult to question. If challenging the prophet means challenging God’s appointed representative, then ordinary accountability becomes spiritually dangerous. The prophetic claim creates a category of authority that sits above normal institutional checks.

Prophecy as a silencing tool. A leader who claims prophetic authority can use that claim to shut down legitimate concern: “God told me this is the direction,” “What you are experiencing is spiritual resistance,” “The enemy is using you to undermine what God is doing here.” These kinds of statements reframe accountability as spiritual attack. The person raising the concern is not simply disagreeing with a leader’s judgment – they are, in this framing, opposing God’s will.

Prophecy as financial leverage. When prophetic words are attached to financial giving – “I see a breakthrough coming for someone who gives sacrificially tonight” – the gift has been converted into a fundraising mechanism. The implication is that divine favor can be accessed through giving, and the prophet is the conduit. This is the intersection of prophecy with prosperity theology, which we will examine in depth in Session 8.

Personal prophecy as control. When a leader gives a congregant a specific “word from God” about their life – their career, their relationships, their future – that word can function as a form of control. The congregant is now navigating their actual life decisions in relation to what the leader said God told them. If the leader’s “word” conflicts with the congregant’s own discernment, the leader’s word carries divine weight. This is a significant power dynamic.

Testing prophecy as a lost practice. Paul is explicit: “test them all” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). “The others should weigh carefully what is said” (1 Corinthians 14:29). But in many communities where prophetic claims carry significant authority, the practice of communal testing has atrophied or disappeared entirely. When testing is gone, the gift loses its communal accountability structure and becomes a tool for whoever claims it most confidently.

Looking ahead to Session 8

Session 8 will take what we have set up here and go deeper into the movements that have been reshaping significant portions of American Christianity:

The New Apostolic Reformation (NAR) – a loosely organized but influential movement that claims to be restoring the governing offices of apostle and prophet, creating a new structure of spiritual authority with significant implications for accountability and church governance.

Dominion theology and the Seven Mountain Mandate – the theological framework that teaches Christians are called to take dominion over the seven spheres of cultural influence (government, business, media, education, family, arts, religion), with significant implications for how the church relates to political power.

Prosperity theology – the teaching that financial blessing is a sign of divine favor and that giving is a mechanism for accessing that favor, with its specific effects on the relationship between leaders and congregants.

We will look at each of these not primarily as theological abstractions but as systems that shape power dynamics in specific ways – and as systems that connect directly to the patterns we have been examining throughout this class.

The question to carry into that session: If a movement’s theological framework makes its leaders harder to question and easier to exploit people, is that a reason to examine the framework more carefully – or is that itself a kind of spiritual resistance?
