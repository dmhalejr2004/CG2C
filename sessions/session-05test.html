Part 1: Narcissism and church leadership

Where the word comes from

The term narcissism comes from the Greek myth of Narcissus – a young man of extraordinary beauty who, upon seeing his own reflection in a pool of water, fell so deeply in love with it that he could not leave. Depending on which version of the myth you read, he either wastes away staring at himself or dies trying to embrace the reflection. The Roman poet Ovid gives the most famous account in the Metamorphoses.

Sigmund Freud introduced the term into clinical psychology in 1914 in his essay “On Narcissism,” using it to describe an excessive self-love that interferes with the capacity to love others. From there it became a central concept in personality psychology and eventually entered the diagnostic vocabulary.

The name itself is useful: narcissism is not primarily about self-confidence or self-belief. It is about being captivated by your own reflection – unable to see others clearly because you are always, in some sense, looking at yourself.

What narcissism actually is

In academic psychology, narcissism is understood as a personality trait – or cluster of traits – that exists on a spectrum in the general population. It is not a binary where you either have it or you do not. Almost everyone has some degree of narcissistic traits, and the question is where someone falls on the continuum and how those traits interact with their context and role.

The core features of narcissism as a personality trait include an inflated sense of self-importance and superiority, a strong need for admiration and external validation, a sense of entitlement – the belief that special treatment is deserved – exploitativeness and a tendency to use others to achieve one’s own goals, lack of empathy or empathy that is instrumental rather than genuine, envy of others alongside a belief that others envy them, and arrogance and grandiosity in presentation.

Researchers have identified two distinct forms worth knowing:

Grandiose narcissism is the most visible and most studied form. It is characterized by extraversion, dominance, and a confident, charismatic surface. Grandiose narcissists tend to seek the spotlight, are comfortable with conflict, and often project an image of strength and certainty.

Vulnerable narcissism is less visible but equally important. It is characterized by hypersensitivity to criticism, a fragile sense of self that requires constant protection, introversion, and a kind of wounded grandiosity – the belief that one deserves more than one is getting. Vulnerable narcissists can appear humble on the surface while harboring deep convictions of special status and deep resentment of perceived slights.

Both forms show up in church leadership, and the vulnerable form is often harder to identify precisely because it does not look like what people expect narcissism to look like.

Narcissistic Personality Disorder – a different category

Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD) is a clinical diagnosis in the DSM-5 and represents the extreme end of the narcissism spectrum. It is estimated to affect roughly 1-6% of the general population, with higher prevalence in men.

To meet the diagnostic threshold for NPD, a person must show a pervasive pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration, and lack of empathy beginning by early adulthood and present across contexts. It causes significant impairment in relationships and functioning.

The distinction between narcissistic traits and NPD matters for two reasons. First, it is not clinically or ethically appropriate to diagnose someone from a distance – including a pastor or church leader – with a personality disorder. Second, significant harm can be caused by people who have strong narcissistic traits but do not meet the full clinical threshold for NPD. The diagnosis is not required for the damage to be real.

Narcissism and leadership – what the research shows

This is where the research becomes genuinely interesting, and directly relevant to the church context.

Narcissism predicts emergent leadership. Multiple studies have found that people with higher narcissism scores are more likely to emerge as leaders in groups. They tend to talk more, project confidence, are comfortable taking charge, and create an impression of competence and vision. In the initial stages of a group or organization, narcissistic individuals are often perceived as natural leaders.

Narcissism does not predict leadership effectiveness. When researchers look at actual outcomes – organizational health, team performance, member wellbeing, long-term results – the relationship between narcissism and effectiveness is much weaker and often negative. The qualities that help someone emerge as a leader are not the same qualities that make someone a good leader over time.

The inverted-U shape. Some researchers have proposed a more nuanced picture: that moderate levels of narcissism may actually be associated with better leadership outcomes than either very low or very high levels. The idea is that some narcissistic traits – confidence, comfort with attention, willingness to make bold decisions – can be genuinely useful in a leader. It is at the high end of the spectrum where the problems become dominant and the costs outweigh the benefits.

The selection problem. The research points to a significant structural issue: the environments that produce and promote leaders – including churches – tend to select for the very traits that predict emergence rather than the traits that predict effectiveness. A church that grows rapidly under a charismatic, confident, visionary pastor may be experiencing exactly the kind of emergent-leadership effect that research describes – without yet encountering the costs that tend to accumulate over time.

Why narcissism is particularly dangerous in church leadership

The church context amplifies the risks of narcissistic leadership in several specific ways.

The structure of worship and preaching creates a built-in admiration supply. A pastor stands at the front every week, receives attention and deference, is thanked and affirmed, and has their words treated as spiritually significant. For someone with high narcissistic traits, this is an extraordinarily powerful reinforcement environment. And unlike most other leadership contexts, very little structural correction is built in.

Theological language provides cover. The language of calling, anointing, divine vision, and spiritual authority can be used – consciously or not – to place a leader beyond normal accountability. “God told me” is very difficult to argue with. “The Lord has laid this on my heart” forecloses disagreement in ways that “I think we should” does not.

The community provides a ready supply of people with needs. Congregations contain people who are hurting, searching, or looking for belonging. Narcissistic leaders are often skilled at identifying and meeting these needs in the short term – creating intense loyalty in people who feel genuinely seen and helped. This creates a protective community around the leader that is hard to penetrate with outside information.

Success is interpreted as divine endorsement. When a church grows, this is often read – by the leader and the community – as evidence that God is blessing what is happening. Growth becomes theological proof. This makes it very difficult to raise concerns about patterns of leadership behavior, because the response is simply: look at what God is doing here.

What narcissistic leadership looks like over time

The early stages often feel electric. A narcissistic leader can be genuinely inspiring, energizing, and vision-casting. People feel part of something significant. The leader seems to have extraordinary gifts.

Over time, characteristic patterns tend to emerge. The inner circle shrinks to people who offer uncritical loyalty. Critics and questioners are marginalized, pushed out, or publicly shamed. The leader’s needs – financial, emotional, logistical – become increasingly central to the community’s life. Accountability structures are weakened or eliminated. The leader’s vision becomes indistinguishable from God’s will in the community’s language. People who leave are described in negative spiritual terms. Empathy is performative – present on stage, absent in private.

How prevalent is narcissism in pastors?

Direct research on narcissism specifically in pastoral populations is limited, but what exists is sobering. Studies of clergy have found that the profession attracts individuals with above-average narcissistic traits – which makes intuitive sense given that ministry involves regular public performance, admiration from a congregation, a sense of special calling, and authority over others’ spiritual lives. These are conditions that appeal to and reinforce narcissistic needs.

Anecdotal and qualitative research from people who have experienced spiritual abuse consistently describes patterns that map onto narcissistic leadership: the inability to admit error, the exploitation of congregants’ vulnerabilities, the use of spiritual authority to silence critics, and the centrality of the leader’s own narrative and needs in the community’s life.

The broader research on narcissism in leadership contexts suggests it is significantly higher than in the general population – partly because of selection effects (narcissistic individuals seek leadership roles) and partly because leadership environments reinforce narcissistic traits over time.

Part 2: Prophecy as a claim to leadership

What prophecy means in the New Testament

We touched on prophecy in Session 4. It is worth going deeper here because the misuse of prophetic language as a claim to authority is one of the most consistently documented dynamics in church leadership failure.

In the New Testament, the Greek word propheteuo – to prophesy – and its related noun prophetes – prophet – appear frequently. The most sustained treatment of prophecy as a congregational gift is in 1 Corinthians 12-14, and Paul’s description there is specific enough to be worth examining carefully.

1 Corinthians 14:3 gives the clearest functional definition: “But the one who prophesies speaks to people for their strengthening, encouraging and comfort.”

That is the primary New Testament purpose of prophecy in the gathered community: strengthening, encouragement, and comfort. It is directed toward the community’s benefit, not the prophet’s authority.

Prophecy is subject to communal testing. 1 Corinthians 14:29 – “Two or three prophets should speak, and the others should weigh carefully what is said.” This is the most important structural point. In the New Testament, prophetic speech is not self-authenticating. It is subject to communal discernment. The community weighs what is said. No prophetic claim places itself above examination.

Prophecy is a gift, not an office. The New Testament does not describe a prophetic office that confers ongoing authority over a congregation. Ephesians 4:11 lists prophets as one of the gifts Christ gave to the church, but the purpose is equipping – not ruling. 1 Corinthians 12 describes prophecy as one gift among many, distributed by the Spirit as the Spirit chooses, for the common good. It is not a rank. It is not a credential. It is not a claim to leadership.

The work of the Spirit in our lives is broader than prophecy. The New Testament describes the Spirit’s work in forming character (Galatians 5:22-23), equipping believers with varied gifts (1 Corinthians 12), and interceding in prayer (Romans 8:26). This work is distributed across the whole community. No single individual holds a monopoly on the Spirit’s activity, and the Spirit’s work in a person’s life does not translate into authority over others.

The Old Testament prophetic office is distinct. The New Testament’s use of prophetic language builds on but differs from the Old Testament model. Old Testament prophets like Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel held a specific office with national and covenantal implications. Their words were preserved as Scripture. The New Testament explicitly places the apostles and Old Testament prophets as the foundation of the church (Ephesians 2:20) – a completed, foundational role. The congregational prophetic gift described in 1 Corinthians operates differently: it is occasional, subject to testing, and aimed at edification rather than governance.

How prophecy gets used as a claim to authority

The gap between what the New Testament describes and what gets practiced in many contemporary churches is significant.

The anointed leader. In many charismatic and Pentecostal traditions, and increasingly in broader evangelical settings, a leader may be understood as uniquely anointed – set apart by God’s direct choice and supernatural gifting in a way that confers special authority. The leader’s decisions, vision, and direction are understood as flowing from this divine anointing rather than from ordinary leadership capacity or community discernment.

“God told me.” When a leader regularly uses the language of direct divine communication – “God told me we should…” or “The Lord has shown me…” – this functions as a way of foreclosing discussion. If God told the leader, then disagreeing with the leader becomes disagreeing with God. This is a very old and very effective mechanism for protecting authority from challenge.

Personal prophecy as control. In some contexts, leaders or their inner circles deliver personal prophetic words to congregation members about their futures, callings, or relationships. When these words are tied to compliance with the leader’s agenda – “God is calling you to serve here” or “I sense that the Lord is saying this relationship is not right for you” – prophecy becomes a mechanism of control rather than edification.

The prophet-leader fusion. The New Testament keeps prophecy and leadership as distinct categories. A congregational gift of prophecy does not confer leadership authority. But in many contemporary settings the two are fused: the leader is also understood as the primary prophet, whose words carry direct divine weight. This removes the communal testing that the New Testament requires and places the leader in a functionally unaccountable position.

The New Apostolic Reformation and the Seven Mountains

One of the most significant and least understood movements shaping contemporary evangelical and charismatic Christianity is what scholars call the New Apostolic Reformation (NAR). It is worth understanding because its influence extends well beyond communities that would use that label.

The NAR holds that God is restoring the offices of apostle and prophet to the church in our time – not as gifts distributed throughout the congregation, but as authoritative offices held by specific designated individuals. These apostles and prophets hold governing authority over the church and, in the NAR framework, over society.

The “Seven Mountains” or “Seven Mountain Mandate” is a related teaching that holds that Christians are called to claim dominion over seven spheres of society: religion, family, education, government, media, arts and entertainment, and business. The language of dominion and authority is central. The goal is not simply Christian witness in these spheres but Christian control of them.

The NAR framework does several things directly relevant to the questions this class is asking. It restores the apostolic and prophetic offices as governing authorities, which removes the New Testament’s structural safeguard of communal testing. It fuses spiritual gifting with institutional power. It provides theological justification for leaders who claim direct divine authority. And it frames challenge to that authority as spiritual rebellion.

The NAR is not a formal organization with membership rolls. It is a network of influence, and its ideas have percolated into many congregations that would not identify with it directly. The elevation of apostles and prophets as governing authorities, the language of the anointed leader, the dominion theology of the Seven Mountains – these ideas show up in settings that have no formal connection to NAR leadership.

The New Testament’s vision looks quite different. The foundational apostolic role belonged to those who personally witnessed the risen Christ and were directly commissioned by him – a historically completed role. The prophetic gift is congregational, occasional, and subject to testing. Neither of these functions as a governing office that places an individual beyond communal accountability.

Part 3: When leadership fails – what to do

When does it become a failure?

Not every mistake a leader makes is a failure. Leaders in any context make errors of judgment, misstep in relationships, and get things wrong. The New Testament is not naive about human imperfection, and a framework for evaluating leadership needs to distinguish between categories.

Imperfection is ordinary human error, miscommunication, poor decisions made in good faith, and genuine mistakes that are acknowledged and addressed.

Ongoing pattern refers to repeated behaviors that reveal something about character rather than circumstance. A single loss of temper is imperfection. A consistent pattern of using anger to silence people is a character issue.

Moral failure is a specific serious violation – often of sexual ethics, financial integrity, or truthfulness – that represents a breach of the qualifications for leadership the New Testament describes.

Abuse is the use of power to harm people. This is categorically different from imperfection or even moral failure. Abuse involves a power differential being exploited. It may be sexual, spiritual, emotional, financial, or physical. It is not simply doing wrong. It is doing wrong in a way that causes harm to vulnerable people.

The difference between church hurt and abuse is important and worth naming carefully. Church hurt is the pain that comes from any of the above – imperfect communities, poor decisions, moral failures, genuine disagreements. It is real and it deserves to be acknowledged. Abuse is a specific category of harm where power is used to exploit, control, or damage. Not all church hurt is abuse. All abuse causes church hurt. The distinction matters because the appropriate response differs significantly.

Why leadership failure is so impactful

When leadership fails in a church context, the impact tends to be disproportionately large. This is not an accident or a sign of people being overly sensitive. There are structural reasons why church leadership failure hits differently.

The trust involved is unusually high. People come to churches in moments of genuine vulnerability – grief, crisis, searching, transition. They extend a level of trust to church leaders that they would not extend in most other contexts. When that trust is violated, the wound is deep precisely because the investment was real.

The spiritual dimensions compound the harm. When a leader’s authority is understood as spiritually derived – when they represent God, speak for God, or are understood as God’s representative – their failure raises questions that go beyond the human. Did God fail? Was my faith misplaced? Is the whole framework wrong? The harm is not just relational but theological.

The community often becomes an extension of the harm. In many cases of leadership failure, the congregation or its leaders respond in ways that compound the original harm – denying it happened, minimizing it, prioritizing the institution’s reputation, or pressuring victims to be silent for the sake of unity. The people who should be a resource for healing become a second source of harm.

The secrecy that often surrounds it isolates victims. Church communities can be very small social worlds. When someone has been harmed and the community rallies around the leader, the victim can find themselves suddenly without their entire support network. The social cost of speaking is enormous.

What to do about it

The response to leadership failure should be proportionate to the category of failure.

For imperfection: honest conversation, clear feedback, expectation of acknowledgment and growth. Most leadership communities can handle this well if they have built the relational trust and structural accountability to do so.

For ongoing patterns: more formal accountability – conversations with a board, elder team, or denominational structure. Clear naming of what is being observed. Concrete expectations with consequences. Time-limited monitoring with agreed-upon benchmarks.

For moral failure: this depends significantly on the nature of the failure, who was harmed, and whether harm to others is involved. The New Testament’s framework from 1 Timothy 5:19-20 is relevant: accusations require corroboration, but confirmed sin is addressed publicly, not privately managed.

For abuse: the response is not primarily a church matter. Abuse requires outside accountability – civil authorities where laws have been broken, professional counseling for those harmed, and in many cases legal reporting obligations. The church does not have the expertise, objectivity, or authority to adjudicate abuse internally. Attempts to handle abuse entirely internally almost always cause further harm.

What to do when there is no recognition of failure

This is one of the hardest scenarios: a leader who has caused real harm but who does not acknowledge it, and a community that continues to rally around them – often because the church is growing, the leader remains compelling, and the costs of acknowledging the problem feel too high.

Growth is not evidence of God’s blessing on the leader’s character. Churches can grow for many reasons, including the natural human attraction to confident, charismatic leaders – which, as we saw in the narcissism section, is not the same thing as effective or healthy leadership. The New Testament’s fruit test is not about attendance. It is about what is being produced in people’s lives.

The community’s loyalty may be protecting harm. People who are deeply formed by a community and a leader will often go to significant lengths to explain away evidence of failure. This is not stupidity or weakness. It is the predictable result of strong social bonds, significant personal investment, and the genuine good that has also been present. Recognizing this can reduce the self-blame many harm survivors experience when they ask themselves why they did not see it sooner.

You may not be able to fix it. This is painful but important. You cannot force a leader to acknowledge failure. You cannot force a community to recognize harm it is not ready to see. What you can do is name what you have experienced clearly, seek support outside the community, and make your own decisions about participation and continued relationship.

External accountability is often the only path. Denominational structures, outside investigators, civil authorities, journalism, and public accountability have historically been the primary mechanisms that produce recognition of failure in church contexts where internal accountability has failed. If internal paths have been exhausted, external ones are legitimate.

What to do when people keep following a failed leader

This is a companion question that many people in this room may be sitting with. People you love, communities you care about, continuing to follow and defend a leader you know or believe to have caused harm.

People rarely leave a community or a leader because someone told them to. Change tends to happen through their own accumulating experience over time. Your job is not to rescue people from their choices – that approach tends to push people closer to the community rather than toward the door.

What you can do is stay in honest relationship with people you care about. Name what you see without issuing ultimatums. Ask curious questions rather than making accusations. Be a presence of honest friendship that does not require agreement.

And you can grieve the reality that some people you love may choose differently than you would choose. That grief is real and it deserves space.

What does restoration look like?

The New Testament’s posture toward people who fail is consistently oriented toward restoration. Galatians 6:1 – “restore that person gently.” 2 Corinthians 2 – Paul urges the Corinthians to reaffirm their love for a person who has been disciplined. The goal is not punishment. It is restoration.

But restoration needs to be understood carefully, because the word is often used in ways that short-circuit the process.

Restoration to the community is different from restoration to leadership. A person who has committed a moral failure or caused harm can be restored to full membership and belonging in a Christian community without being restored to a leadership role. These are two different things, and conflating them causes significant harm to those who were hurt.

Restoration requires genuine acknowledgment. You cannot restore what has not been named. A leader who has not genuinely acknowledged what they did, demonstrated understanding of how it harmed others, and shown sustained change over time has not done the work that restoration requires. Restoration is not a process that happens to someone because enough time has passed. It is a process that happens because something has genuinely changed.

Some failures are permanently disqualifying from leadership. The New Testament’s qualifications for elders and overseers include being “above reproach” and having “a good reputation with outsiders.” Some failures – particularly sexual abuse of minors, patterns of financial fraud, or sustained spiritual abuse – so thoroughly violate these qualifications that restoration to a position of authority over vulnerable people is not appropriate regardless of personal repentance. Repentance and restoration to community are possible. Restoration to leadership is a different question.

Restoration takes time. Any process that moves quickly – that restores a leader to a platform within months of a disclosed failure – should be viewed with significant skepticism. Genuine character change, rebuilding of trust, and demonstration of sustained transformation take years, not months.

The thread connecting all three

Narcissism, the misuse of prophecy, and leadership failure are not three unrelated topics. They are three expressions of the same underlying dynamic: the confusion of the leader’s self with the mission, the community, and God.

When a narcissistic leader enters a church, the leader’s needs gradually become indistinguishable from the church’s needs. When prophetic language is used as a claim to authority, the leader’s voice becomes indistinguishable from God’s voice. When failure happens and is not acknowledged, the leader’s reputation becomes indistinguishable from the community’s identity.

The New Testament’s consistent counter to all three is the same: plural leadership, communal discernment, the fruit test applied over time, and the willingness to confront what is true even when it is costly.

None of that is easy. All of it is necessary.
